Kafka Internals, Message Queues & Event-Driven Architecture

Most Spring Boot engineers use Kafka through @KafkaListener and KafkaTemplate and only meet its internals during an incident: a rebalance storm, a lagging consumer group, an "out of order" bug or a lost message after a broker failure. This chapter opens the box. It starts with the architectural patterns that make events worth having, contrasts traditional queues with distributed logs, and then walks the full path of a record through Kafka: partitioning, batching, the on-disk segment format, replication and the high watermark, consumer groups and their rebalance protocols, transactions, KRaft, compaction, and the operational practices that keep a cluster healthy.

  • Prerequisites: Chapter 5 (replication & partitioning), Chapter 6 (outbox, idempotency, exactly-once myth); basic Spring Kafka usage.
  • You will be able to: choose between event notification, event-carried state transfer, event sourcing and CQRS; choose between RabbitMQ, SQS/SNS, Pulsar and Kafka; explain how producers batch, partition and retry without reordering; explain segment and index files; reason about ISR, high watermark and min.insync.replicas for durability; compare eager, cooperative and KIP-848 rebalancing; implement at-least-once and exactly-once pipelines; design retry topics and DLQs; evolve schemas safely; and size and monitor a cluster.

1. Introduction: Why Logs Ate Messaging

A synchronous REST call couples two services in time: the caller waits, and if the callee is slow or down, the caller is too. An event-driven design inverts that dependency. The producer records a fact ("order 42 was placed") and moves on; any number of consumers react to it on their own schedule. That decoupling in time, in space (producer does not know consumers) and in scale (consumers scale independently) is why event-driven architecture underpins most large backends.

Apache Kafka became the dominant backbone for this style because it is not a queue in the traditional sense. It is a partitioned, replicated, append-only log. Messages are not deleted when consumed; each consumer group keeps its own position (offset). That one design decision gives Kafka properties that classic brokers struggle with: replay of history, many independent consumers of the same data at no extra broker cost, ordering within a partition, very high sequential-I/O throughput, and the ability to serve as a durable source of truth for stream processing and change data capture.

In the AI era Kafka's role has grown rather than shrunk. Feature pipelines for real-time ML, streaming ingestion into vector stores for retrieval-augmented generation, model-inference request queues, clickstream and telemetry for evaluation, and agent-event audit logs are all typically built on a log. When an LLM service needs fresh product data, the path is usually database → Debezium → Kafka → embedding worker → vector index.

Bridging from Java & Spring Boot

What you already know
JMS (JmsTemplate, @JmsListener), Spring AMQP with RabbitMQ (RabbitTemplate, @RabbitListener), and Spring for Apache Kafka (KafkaTemplate, @KafkaListener, ConcurrentKafkaListenerContainerFactory). Spring's ApplicationEventPublisher and @EventListener for in-process events.
Why that is not enough
Spring Kafka hides partitions, offsets, rebalances and batching behind annotations, but every production property you care about (ordering, duplicate rate, lag, data loss on broker failure, pause during deploys) is decided by those hidden mechanisms and their configuration. JMS mental models ("the broker deletes a message after ack", "competing consumers scale freely") are wrong for Kafka and lead to design bugs.
The modern approach
Treat topics as durable, schema-governed data products. Key records by the entity whose order matters; size partitions for parallelism; use idempotent producers with acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2; use cooperative or KIP-848 rebalancing; commit offsets after processing; make consumers idempotent; route poison messages to retry topics and DLQs; evolve schemas with a registry; monitor lag and under-replicated partitions.
Where Python and Go fit
Python ML and data services usually consume with confluent-kafka (librdkafka-based). Go services use franz-go (pure Go, feature-complete) or confluent-kafka-go. All clients share the same protocol and semantics, so the configuration lessons in this chapter apply directly.
In production
Managed services (Confluent Cloud, Amazon MSK, Aiven, Redpanda as a Kafka-API-compatible alternative) or self-run clusters on Kubernetes with Strimzi; Schema Registry; Kafka Connect with Debezium for CDC; Kafka Streams or Flink for stream processing; lag monitoring with Burrow, kafka-exporter or vendor tools.
Use it when / avoid it when
Use Kafka for high-throughput event streams, multiple independent consumers, replay, CDC and stream processing. Prefer RabbitMQ or SQS for per-message task queues with complex routing, per-message TTL/priority and low volume, or when you have no operational capacity for Kafka. Do not use Kafka as a request/response RPC transport or a general database.

3. Fundamentals

3.1 Events, commands and messages

A command asks a specific recipient to do something and may be rejected ("ReserveStock"). An event states that something has happened; it is immutable, named in the past tense ("StockReserved"), and the producer does not know or care who listens. A message is the envelope that carries either. Confusing the two leads to "events" that are really disguised RPC calls with hidden coupling. A useful test: if the producer would break when nobody consumes the message, it is a command.

3.2 Four patterns that all get called "event-driven"

Martin Fowler's 2017 essay distinguishes patterns that teams conflate. Knowing which one you are using changes schema design, retention and coupling.

Event notification

The event is a thin signal: {"type":"CustomerAddressChanged","customerId":"c-9"}. Consumers that need details call back the producer's API. Low coupling of schemas, but consumers depend on the producer's availability at processing time, and a burst of events produces a burst of callbacks ("thundering herd").

Event-carried state transfer (ECST)

The event carries the state consumers need: the full new address. Consumers maintain their own local replica (a read model) and never call back. This maximises availability and autonomy at the cost of data duplication and larger, more tightly specified schemas. Compacted Kafka topics keyed by entity id are the natural implementation: a new consumer can bootstrap the latest state of every entity by reading the compacted topic from the beginning.

Event sourcing

The events are the system of record. An aggregate's current state is computed by folding its event history (OrderPlaced, ItemAdded, ItemRemoved, OrderPaid). You gain a perfect audit trail, temporal queries and the ability to rebuild new projections from history; you pay with event schema evolution forever, snapshotting for long streams, and eventual consistency for reads. Event stores (EventStoreDB, Axon Server) or a relational table per aggregate stream are typical. Kafka is a good distribution log for event-sourced systems but a weak primary event store, because it lacks per-aggregate optimistic concurrency ("append if the stream is at version 17").

CQRS

Command Query Responsibility Segregation separates the write model (validates commands, enforces invariants) from one or more read models optimised for queries (denormalised views in Elasticsearch, Redis, a reporting database). Read models are updated from events, so they lag. CQRS does not require event sourcing, and event sourcing almost always uses CQRS. Use it when read and write shapes differ greatly or scale very differently, not by default.

Spring angle

Spring Modulith's @ApplicationModuleListener plus its Event Publication Registry is an in-process outbox: events published inside a transaction are recorded in a table and delivered after commit, and can be externalised to Kafka. It is a gentle way to start event-driven design inside a modular monolith before splitting services (Chapter 8).

3.4 RabbitMQ and the AMQP 0-9-1 model

Producers publish to an exchange, never directly to a queue. Bindings connect exchanges to queues with a routing rule. Exchange types:

  • Direct: routes to queues whose binding key equals the message's routing key.
  • Fanout: copies to every bound queue (pub/sub).
  • Topic: pattern match on dot-separated routing keys (order.*.eu, order.#).
  • Headers: match on message header values.

Consumers receive pushed messages up to a prefetch limit (basic.qos), process them, and send basic.ack; basic.nack/reject with requeue=false routes to a dead-letter exchange if configured. Unacked messages are redelivered when the channel closes (with the redelivered flag set). Publisher confirms give the producer an ack once the broker has taken responsibility for the message. For replicated durability, use quorum queues (Raft-based replication); classic mirrored queues were removed in RabbitMQ 4.0. RabbitMQ also offers streams, an append-only log type with offset-based consumption, for Kafka-like replay. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026

Where RabbitMQ shines: rich routing, per-message TTL and priority, request/reply patterns, delayed retries via dead-letter loops, modest volumes and many small queues. Spring AMQP's SimpleMessageListenerContainer, RetryInterceptorBuilder and RepublishMessageRecoverer are its idioms.

3.5 Amazon SQS and SNS

SQS standard queues give at-least-once delivery and best-effort ordering with nearly unlimited throughput. A received message becomes invisible for the visibility timeout; if not deleted in time it reappears for another consumer, so long processing must extend the timeout. A redrive policy moves messages to a DLQ after maxReceiveCount receives. FIFO queues provide ordering per message group id and deduplicate by deduplication id within a 5-minute window; throughput is lower and parallelism comes from many message groups. SNS is pub/sub fan-out; the classic pattern is SNS topic → multiple SQS queues so each subscriber gets its own durable queue. EventBridge adds content-based routing and schema discovery. Choose SQS/SNS when you are on AWS, want zero broker operations, and do not need replay or stream processing. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026

3.6 Apache Pulsar, briefly

Pulsar separates serving (stateless brokers) from storage (Apache BookKeeper "bookies" storing ledger segments). Because brokers hold no partition data, rebalancing a topic to another broker does not copy data, and storage scales independently. It has native multi-tenancy (tenants/namespaces), geo-replication, tiered storage, and four subscription types on the same topic: exclusive, failover, shared (queue semantics with per-message acks) and key_shared (queue semantics with per-key ordering). The trade-off is operating more components (brokers, bookies, a metadata store) and a smaller ecosystem than Kafka's.

3.7 Kafka's core vocabulary

  • Record: key (optional bytes), value (bytes), headers, timestamp. Kafka itself is schema-agnostic; serialisation is the client's job.
  • Topic: a named stream, split into partitions. Each partition is an ordered, immutable sequence of records, each with a monotonically increasing offset.
  • Broker: a server that stores partition replicas and serves clients. A partition has one leader replica (serves writes and, by default, reads) and zero or more followers.
  • Replication factor (RF): number of replicas per partition, typically 3.
  • ISR (in-sync replicas): replicas currently caught up with the leader. Only ISR members are eligible to become leader under clean election.
  • Producer: client that appends records; chooses the partition; batches and compresses.
  • Consumer group: a set of consumers sharing a group.id; each partition is assigned to exactly one member of the group at a time. Different groups consume independently.
  • Committed offset: per group and partition, the offset of the next record to consume, stored in the internal compacted topic __consumer_offsets.
  • Controller: the component that manages cluster metadata (topics, partition leadership, ISR changes). In Kafka 4.x it is a Raft quorum of controller nodes (KRaft) storing metadata in the __cluster_metadata log.

3.8 Ordering guarantees

Kafka guarantees order within a partition, never across partitions. Records with the same key go to the same partition (with the default partitioner and a constant partition count), so per-key order is preserved. Three things silently break per-key ordering: increasing the partition count (keys re-map to different partitions), producer retries with unsafe settings (section 4.2), and consumers that process records of one partition concurrently (e.g., handing them to a thread pool). Choose the key as the entity whose events must be ordered: orderId, accountId, deviceId.

3.9 Delivery semantics

  • At-most-once: commit the offset before processing. A crash after commit loses the record.
  • At-least-once: process, then commit. A crash after processing but before commit reprocesses records. The default and correct choice for most services, paired with idempotent processing.
  • Exactly-once (effectively-once): Kafka transactions commit produced output records and consumed offsets atomically, and read_committed consumers never see aborted output. Valid for Kafka-to-Kafka pipelines (Kafka Streams exactly_once_v2). For external side effects, use idempotency (Chapter 6).

3.10 Retention and log compaction

Delete policy (cleanup.policy=delete): whole segments are deleted when older than retention.ms (default 7 days) or when the partition exceeds retention.bytes. Compact policy (cleanup.policy=compact): Kafka keeps at least the latest record for each key; older records with the same key are eventually removed, and a record with a null value (a tombstone) deletes the key after delete.retention.ms. Compacted topics act as changelogs or key-value snapshots: __consumer_offsets, Kafka Streams state-store changelogs, ECST entity topics, and Kafka Connect's config/offset topics are all compacted. compact,delete combines both.

3.11 Schemas and evolution

Because events outlive the code that wrote them, their schemas must evolve compatibly. A schema registry (Confluent Schema Registry, Apicurio, AWS Glue Schema Registry) stores versioned Avro, Protobuf or JSON Schema definitions per subject (by default <topic>-value). Serializers register or look up the schema and prefix each payload with a small schema id (in Confluent's wire format: a magic byte plus a 4-byte id); deserializers fetch the writer schema by id and resolve it against their reader schema. Compatibility modes govern which changes are allowed: BACKWARD (new readers can read old data; the Confluent default), FORWARD (old readers can read new data), FULL (both), and their _TRANSITIVE variants (checked against all previous versions, not just the latest).

4. Internal Working: The Life of a Record

4.1 The producer pipeline

When your code calls kafkaTemplate.send(topic, key, value), very little happens on the calling thread:

  1. Metadata. The producer needs the partition count and leaders for the topic. It fetches cluster metadata on first use and refreshes it periodically (metadata.max.age.ms) or after errors such as NOT_LEADER_OR_FOLLOWER. If metadata is unavailable, send() blocks up to max.block.ms (default 60 s). Many "the service hangs on startup" incidents are this.
  2. Serialisation. Key and value serializers turn objects into bytes (Avro/Protobuf serializers also talk to the schema registry, cached after the first call).
  3. Partitioning. If you set an explicit partition, it is used. If the record has a key, the default strategy computes murmur2(keyBytes) mod numPartitions (as a positive int). If the key is null, the built-in uniform sticky behaviour (KIP-794, Kafka 3.3+) sends records to one partition until a batch fills, then switches, preferring less-loaded brokers. Sticky partitioning creates fuller batches than naive round-robin.
  4. Accumulation. The record is appended to an in-memory RecordAccumulator, which keeps a deque of batches per partition. A batch is at most batch.size bytes (default 16 KB). The total memory is capped by buffer.memory (default 32 MB); when full, send() blocks up to max.block.ms. send() then returns a future.
  5. Sender thread. A background I/O thread drains batches that are full, or whose linger.ms has elapsed (default 5 ms since Kafka 4.0, previously 0), groups them by destination broker into a single ProduceRequest, compresses each batch (compression.type: none, gzip, snappy, lz4, zstd), and sends it. At most max.in.flight.requests.per.connection unacknowledged requests (default 5) are outstanding per broker connection. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026
  6. Acknowledgement. The broker responds according to acks: 0 (no response; fire and forget), 1 (leader has appended to its log), or all/-1 (all current ISR members have the record; default since Kafka 3.0). The future completes and callbacks run on the sender thread (so never block inside a send callback).
  7. Retries. Retriable errors (leader change, timeouts, not enough replicas) are retried automatically until delivery.timeout.ms (default 120 s) expires; retries defaults to effectively infinite, bounded by that timeout. Backoff is governed by retry.backoff.ms and retry.backoff.max.ms.

4.2 Idempotent producer and ordering under retries

Without idempotence, a retry can create a duplicate (the first attempt was written but its response was lost) and, with more than one request in flight, can reorder records: batch 1 fails, batch 2 succeeds, batch 1's retry lands after batch 2. The historical fix was max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=1, which kills throughput.

The idempotent producer (default enable.idempotence=true since Kafka 3.0, provided acks=all, retries > 0 and in-flight ≤ 5) solves both. On initialisation the producer obtains a producer id (PID) and epoch. Each batch carries the PID, epoch and a per-partition sequence number. The partition leader remembers the last five batch sequence numbers per PID and:

  • discards a batch whose sequence it has already written (duplicate) while returning success,
  • rejects a batch whose sequence is ahead of the expected next one with OUT_OF_ORDER_SEQUENCE_NUMBER, forcing the producer to resend in order.

This gives exactly-once, in-order writes per partition for the lifetime of one producer session. It does not deduplicate across producer restarts (a new PID) or across application-level re-sends (your code calling send() twice). Producer state is persisted in .snapshot files alongside segments so it survives broker restarts and leader changes.

4.3 The broker's storage layout

Each partition replica is a directory, e.g. /var/kafka/data/orders-3/, containing segments. A segment is a set of files named by the base offset of its first record:

orders-3/
  00000000000000000000.log        records (batches), append-only
  00000000000000000000.index      sparse map: relative offset -> byte position
  00000000000000000000.timeindex  sparse map: timestamp -> offset
  00000000000004718273.log        next segment, base offset 4718273
  00000000000004718273.index
  00000000000004718273.timeindex
  00000000000004718273.snapshot   producer state (PID -> last sequences)
  00000000000004718273.txnindex   aborted transactions in this segment
  leader-epoch-checkpoint         epoch -> start offset history
  partition.metadata

Only the newest segment (the active segment) is written. It is rolled when it reaches segment.bytes (default 1 GiB) or segment.ms elapses. Retention and compaction operate on closed segments only, which is why a low-traffic topic with a 1 GiB segment may retain data far beyond retention.ms.

The .log file stores record batches exactly as producers sent them (the v2 format: a batch header with base offset, length, partition leader epoch, CRC, attributes for compression and transactional/control flags, producer id/epoch, base sequence, then the possibly-compressed records with varint-encoded deltas for offset and timestamp). Because the broker stores the producer's compressed bytes as-is (unless the topic's compression.type differs), it avoids recompression, and it can send them to consumers with sendfile/zero-copy from the OS page cache when TLS is not in use.

The .index file is sparse: an entry is added roughly every index.interval.bytes (default 4 KiB) of log data. Each entry is 8 bytes: a 4-byte offset relative to the segment base and a 4-byte file position. Index files are memory-mapped. To serve a fetch at offset o, the broker (1) finds the segment whose base offset is the greatest ≤ o (a skip-list/floor lookup over segments), (2) binary-searches the index for the greatest entry ≤ o, and (3) scans forward in the log from that position, at most a few KiB. The .timeindex works the same way for offsetsForTimes lookups (e.g., "reset group to 2 hours ago") and time-based retention.

Insight: why Kafka is fast

Sequential appends to the page cache, batching end to end (producer batch = on-disk batch = fetch response), compression of whole batches, zero-copy transfer, and an O(1)-ish lookup via sparse mmap'd indexes. Kafka does not fsync each message; durability comes from replication across brokers, with flushing left to the OS by default.

4.4 Replication: leaders, followers, ISR and the high watermark

Followers replicate by sending Fetch requests to the leader, exactly like consumers. The leader tracks each follower's fetch position (its log end offset, LEO).

  • ISR membership. A follower stays in the ISR if it has caught up to the leader's LEO within replica.lag.time.max.ms (default 30 s). A follower that falls behind longer is removed from the ISR (the leader proposes the change to the controller); it rejoins when it catches up.
  • High watermark (HW). The leader advances the HW to the minimum LEO across the ISR. Records below the HW are committed: present on every ISR replica. Consumers can only read up to the HW (more precisely, up to the last stable offset for read_committed consumers, section 4.9). This prevents a consumer from reading a record that could vanish in a leader failover.
  • acks=all semantics. The leader responds to the producer when the record is below the HW, i.e., when every current ISR member has it. If the ISR has shrunk to just the leader, acks=all degrades to acks=1. That is why min.insync.replicas exists.
  • min.insync.replicas (topic or broker config, default 1). With acks=all, if ISR size is below this value the leader rejects writes with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS. The standard durable configuration is RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all: every acknowledged write is on at least two brokers, and the partition remains writable with one broker down.
  • Leader epochs. Each leadership change increments the partition's leader epoch. After a failover, a former leader or lagging follower uses the epoch history (OffsetsForLeaderEpoch) to find where its log diverged from the new leader's and truncates there, instead of the older, unsafe HW-based truncation (KIP-101).

Unclean leader election

If every ISR member is dead and only an out-of-sync replica survives, Kafka can either wait for an ISR member to return (partition offline: unavailable but consistent) or elect the out-of-sync replica (unclean.leader.election.enable=true: available, but acknowledged records it lacks are lost and consumers may see offsets reused). The default is false. This is CAP in miniature, configured per topic.

4.5 The controller and KRaft

Before Kafka 4.0, one broker acted as controller and stored cluster metadata in ZooKeeper. Failover required the new controller to load the full state from ZooKeeper, which took minutes on clusters with hundreds of thousands of partitions. KRaft (KIP-500 and follow-ups) replaces this with a controller quorum (typically 3 or 5 nodes, either dedicated or combined with brokers in small deployments) running a Raft variant over a metadata log, __cluster_metadata.

  • The active controller is the Raft leader; standby controllers are hot replicas of the metadata log, so failover is fast.
  • Brokers fetch the metadata log like a topic and apply records (topic created, partition leader changed, ISR changed) to their in-memory metadata image. Metadata propagation is therefore incremental and ordered.
  • Brokers register and send heartbeats to the controller; a broker that misses heartbeats is fenced and its partition leaderships move.
  • Kafka 4.0 removed ZooKeeper mode entirely; clusters must migrate to KRaft on a 3.x bridge release before upgrading. Dynamic controller quorums (KIP-853) allow adding/removing controllers. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026

4.6 The consumer fetch path

A consumer's poll(timeout) loop does three things: participates in group membership (heartbeats on a background thread), sends Fetch requests to partition leaders (or to a nearby follower with rack-aware follower fetching, KIP-392), and returns buffered records to your code. Key settings: fetch.min.bytes and fetch.max.wait.ms (broker holds the request until enough data, trading latency for efficiency), max.partition.fetch.bytes, and max.poll.records (how many records one poll() returns, default 500). If your processing loop does not call poll() within max.poll.interval.ms (default 5 min), the consumer proactively leaves the group, which triggers a rebalance: the most common cause of "rebalance storms" with slow processing.

4.7 Consumer groups and the group coordinator

Each group is managed by a group coordinator: the broker that leads the partition of __consumer_offsets numbered abs(hash(group.id)) mod offsets.topic.num.partitions (default 50 partitions). The coordinator stores membership and committed offsets for the group.

Classic protocol (eager)

  1. Members send JoinGroup. The coordinator waits for all known members (up to the rebalance timeout), picks one as group leader, and sends it the member list and their subscriptions.
  2. The leader runs the configured assignor client-side (range, round-robin, sticky, cooperative-sticky) and sends the assignment in SyncGroup; the coordinator distributes each member's share.
  3. With the eager protocol, every member revokes all its partitions before rejoining: a stop-the-world pause for the whole group, on every member join/leave/crash and every rolling deploy.

Cooperative incremental rebalancing (KIP-429)

With CooperativeStickyAssignor, members keep their partitions during the rebalance. The new assignment is computed; only partitions that must move are revoked, and a follow-up rebalance assigns them to their new owners. Members not affected keep processing throughout. The price is two rebalance rounds for moved partitions.

Static membership (KIP-345)

Setting group.instance.id (e.g., the pod name of a StatefulSet) lets a restarting member rejoin with the same identity within session.timeout.ms and get its partitions back without any rebalance. Ideal for rolling restarts.

The next-generation consumer protocol (KIP-848)

Generally available since Kafka 4.0 and enabled with group.protocol=consumer, KIP-848 moves assignment to the broker. There is no JoinGroup/SyncGroup barrier. Each member sends periodic ConsumerGroupHeartbeat requests carrying its subscription and owned partitions; the coordinator computes a target assignment with a server-side assignor (uniform or range, set with group.remote.assignor or the broker's default) and reconciles each member independently and incrementally: it tells a member to release specific partitions, and assigns them to their new owner only once released. A member joining or leaving no longer pauses the others. Regex subscriptions are evaluated on the broker. Client-side settings such as partition.assignment.strategy, session.timeout.ms and heartbeat.interval.ms become broker-controlled group configs under this protocol. As of Kafka 4.3 the classic protocol remains the default but migration is recommended, with consumer slated to become the default in 5.0; Kafka Streams has its own KIP-848-style protocol (KIP-1071), marked production-ready in 4.2. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026

Share groups (KIP-932, "Queues for Kafka")

A share group lets multiple consumers consume the same partition cooperatively with per-record acknowledgement (accept, release, reject), delivery counts and acquisition locks, i.e., queue semantics without a partition-count ceiling and without per-partition ordering. Production-ready since Kafka 4.2, with DLQ support added in 4.4. Use them for work queues where ordering does not matter. Spring for Apache Kafka 4.0 added share-consumer support. 4.8 Offset commits

A commit writes (group, topic, partition) → offset as a record to __consumer_offsets. Semantics: the committed offset is the next record to read. Options:

  • Auto commit (enable.auto.commit=true, every auto.commit.interval.ms): commits offsets of records returned by previous polls, on the next poll. Safe for at-least-once only if all records from a poll are fully processed before the next poll (true for simple synchronous loops; false if you hand records to other threads).
  • Manual sync/async commit: commitSync() after processing; commitAsync() for throughput with a final commitSync() on shutdown/revocation.
  • Spring Kafka disables client auto-commit and lets the container commit according to AckMode: BATCH (default: after all records from a poll are processed), RECORD, MANUAL/MANUAL_IMMEDIATE (you call Acknowledgment.acknowledge()), and others.

Commits should happen in the onPartitionsRevoked callback during rebalances, so the new owner does not reprocess. Spring's container handles this for you.

4.9 Transactions and exactly-once

A transactional producer is configured with a stable transactional.id. The flow:

  1. initTransactions(): the producer finds its transaction coordinator (the leader of the __transaction_state partition for its transactional id), which bumps the producer epoch, fencing any older "zombie" instance with the same id, and aborts or completes any transaction it left open.
  2. beginTransaction(), then send() records to any partitions. Before the first write to a new partition, the producer registers it with the coordinator (AddPartitionsToTxn; since KIP-890, done implicitly by the broker as part of the produce request in newer versions).
  3. sendOffsetsToTransaction(offsets, groupMetadata) adds the consumer group's offsets to the same transaction.
  4. commitTransaction(): the coordinator writes PREPARE_COMMIT to its log, then writes transaction markers (control records) into every involved partition, including __consumer_offsets, then writes COMPLETE_COMMIT. This is a two-phase commit, but all participants are Kafka partitions managed by one replicated coordinator, so it does not have XA's blocking problems.

Consumers with isolation.level=read_committed read only up to the last stable offset (LSO): the first offset of any still-open transaction. Records of aborted transactions are filtered using the .txnindex. A long-running or hung transaction therefore stalls read_committed consumers of that partition (bounded by transaction.timeout.ms). Kafka Streams with processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2 (KIP-447) uses one transactional producer per stream thread and is the simplest correct way to get exactly-once Kafka-to-Kafka processing.

4.10 Log compaction internals

The log cleaner threads divide a compacted partition into a clean head (already compacted) and a dirty tail. When the dirty ratio exceeds min.cleanable.dirty.ratio (default 0.5), the cleaner scans the dirty section building an in-memory map of key → latest offset, then rewrites segments, keeping only records whose offset equals the latest offset for their key (plus tombstones until delete.retention.ms passes). Offsets are preserved (compaction creates gaps, never renumbers), so consumer positions stay valid. The active segment is never compacted, and min.compaction.lag.ms guarantees a minimum time a record remains uncompacted (useful so consumers see every intermediate update for a while).

4.11 Failure handling: poison pills, retries and DLQs

Because a partition is consumed in order, a record that always fails (a poison pill: malformed payload, schema mismatch, a bug triggered by specific data) blocks every later record in that partition if you simply retry forever. Options:

  • Deserialisation failures: wrap deserializers in Spring's ErrorHandlingDeserializer so the failure surfaces to the error handler rather than looping inside poll().
  • Blocking retries (Spring DefaultErrorHandler with BackOff): retry in place a few times with backoff, then hand the record to a recoverer (log, or DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer to a .DLT topic). Preserves order; blocks the partition during retries.
  • Non-blocking retries with retry topics (Spring @RetryableTopic, Uber's design): on failure, publish the record to orders-retry-0 (consumed after a delay), then orders-retry-1, and finally orders-dlt. The main partition keeps flowing. Order for the failed key is lost, so use this only when per-key order is not required or when you also park later records for the same key.
  • Classify errors: non-retriable (validation, deserialisation) go straight to DLT; transient (timeouts, 503) get retries. Spring's addNotRetryableExceptions and @RetryableTopic(exclude = ...) do this.
  • DLQ operations: a DLQ is useless without tooling to inspect, fix and replay messages, plus alerting on its depth.

4.12 Kafka Connect and Kafka Streams

Kafka Connect is a distributed runtime for source connectors (external system → Kafka: Debezium, JDBC, S3) and sink connectors (Kafka → external: Elasticsearch, S3, JDBC, Snowflake). Workers form a group (using the same group protocol) and distribute connector tasks; configs, offsets and status live in compacted internal topics. Single Message Transforms (SMTs) do light per-record changes; converters handle serialisation. Use Connect instead of writing ad-hoc producer/consumer glue for data integration.

Kafka Streams is a Java library for stream processing inside your application (no separate cluster). A topology of processors reads from topics, transforms, joins and aggregates, and writes to topics. Parallelism follows input partitions (one task per partition group). Stateful operations keep local state stores (RocksDB by default) backed by compacted changelog topics, so state can be restored on another instance. KStream models an event stream; KTable models a changelog (latest value per key); joins and windowed aggregations (tumbling, hopping, sliding, session windows with grace periods for late data) are first-class. Repartition topics are created automatically when you re-key. Apache Flink is the main alternative when you need a separate processing cluster, very large state or SQL-first pipelines.

4.14 Ordering, end to end

Per-key ordering is a chain, and every link must hold:

  1. Source order: events must be produced in the order they happened. With an outbox and multiple relay instances this already fails (Chapter 6, Exercise 4).
  2. Partition choice: same key, same partition count, same partitioner across all producers (a Go producer using a different hash than Java's murmur2 will scatter keys; franz-go and librdkafka offer Java-compatible partitioners, which you must select explicitly in librdkafka with partitioner=murmur2_random).
  3. Producer retries: idempotence on, or in-flight = 1.
  4. Consumption: a partition is processed sequentially, or concurrently only across different keys (key-level parallelism).
  5. Retries: blocking retries preserve order; retry topics do not.
  6. Sink: writes to the destination must not be reordered (e.g., parallel async HTTP calls). Version-guarded upserts (WHERE version < :new) make the sink tolerant of reordering, which is the most robust approach.

Tip

When ordering matters, put an entity version or source sequence in every event and make consumers reject or park stale updates. Then ordering bugs become harmless no-ops rather than silent data corruption.

Deep dive: why ISR shrinkage can cause unavailability rather than data loss

Suppose RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2. A network blip makes both followers miss replica.lag.time.max.ms. The leader shrinks the ISR to itself. Now every acks=all produce fails with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS until a follower catches up, even though all three brokers are alive. This is the system choosing consistency over availability by design. If the followers are slow because the leader's network is saturated (many consumers fetching from it), the problem compounds: replication competes with consumer traffic. Remedies include quotas on consumer bandwidth, follower fetching for consumers, more brokers to spread leaders, and alerting on ISR shrink rate before writes fail.

Reading the diagram: output records and input offsets become visible together via markers. Reading the diagram: failures leave the main topic immediately, so healthy records keep flowing. Each retry topic adds delay by having its consumer pause until the record's due time. 6. Math: Throughput, Partitions, Lag and Sizing

6.1 How many partitions?

Intuition: a partition is the unit of parallelism for consumers and a unit of throughput for producers. If one consumer instance can process \( c \) records/s and the target is \( T \) records/s, you need at least \( T/c \) consumers, hence at least that many partitions. Similarly for producer throughput per partition \( p \). A common rule (from Confluent's guidance):

\[ P \ge \max\left( \frac{T}{p}, \frac{T}{c} \right) \]

then add headroom for growth, because increasing partitions later re-maps keys and breaks per-key ordering during the transition. Example: \( T = 60{,}000 \) records/s, each consumer handles \( c = 2{,}500 \) records/s (it calls a database), producer \( p = 20{,}000 \)/s per partition. \( P \ge \max(3, 24) = 24 \); with 2× headroom choose 48. Illustrative

Too many partitions cost: more open file handles and memory per broker, longer leader elections after a broker failure (bounded far better with KRaft), more producer batches that never fill, and more consumer overhead. Keep per-broker partition counts in the low thousands unless your platform documentation says otherwise.

6.2 Batching and linger

Per-request overhead is roughly constant, so throughput rises with batch size until the network or disk saturates. For a producer sending \( r \) records/s of size \( s \) bytes to one partition, a batch fills in \( t_{fill} = \text{batch.size} / (r \cdot s) \). If \( t_{fill} > \text{linger.ms} \), batches are sent half-empty on the linger timer; raising linger.ms toward \( t_{fill} \) improves compression ratio and throughput at the cost of up to linger.ms extra latency. Example: 2,000 records/s of 500 bytes per partition = 1 MB/s; a 64 KB batch fills in 64 ms. A 5 ms linger sends ~5 KB batches; a 20 ms linger sends ~20 KB batches with better compression. Illustrative

6.3 Consumer lag and time to catch up

Lag for a partition is \( \text{lag} = \text{LEO} - \text{committed offset} \) (or HW/LSO-based, depending on the tool). Time lag is more useful for SLOs: the age of the oldest unprocessed record. If producers write at \( \lambda \) records/s and the group processes at \( \mu \) records/s with current lag \( L \), the time to catch up is

\[ t_{catch} = \frac{L}{\mu - \lambda}, \qquad \mu > \lambda. \]

With \( L = 3.6\times10^{6} \), \( \lambda = 5{,}000 \)/s and \( \mu = 6{,}000 \)/s, catch-up takes 3,600 s: one hour. Scaling consumers only helps up to the partition count. If \( \mu \le \lambda \), lag grows without bound; that is a capacity problem, not a tuning one. Illustrative

6.4 Storage and network sizing

Disk per cluster: \( D = W \cdot R \cdot RF \cdot (1+h) / \text{compression ratio} \), where \( W \) is ingress bytes/s, \( R \) retention seconds, and \( h \) headroom (say 30–40%). Example: 50 MB/s ingress, 7 days, RF=3, 2× compression, 40% headroom: \( 50 \times 604{,}800 \times 3 \times 1.4 / 2 \approx 63.5 \) TB. Network egress per cluster: replication \( W(RF-1) \) plus consumer reads \( W \cdot G \) for \( G \) consumer groups; with \( G=4 \): \( 50 \times (2 + 4) = 300 \) MB/s egress against 50 MB/s ingress. Consumers, not producers, usually dominate network cost; follower fetching from the same AZ (KIP-392) reduces cross-AZ transfer charges. Illustrative

6.5 Durability arithmetic

With RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all, an acknowledged record is lost only if both replicas that held it fail permanently (disk loss) before a third copy is made, or if unclean election is enabled. Availability for writes requires at least 2 of 3 replicas in the ISR: one broker can be down (maintenance) and a second failure stops writes on affected partitions (but loses nothing). With RF=3 and min.insync.replicas=3, any single broker restart blocks writes, which is why 2 is the standard choice.

        p.put(StreamsConfig.BOOTSTRAP_SERVERS_CONFIG, "localhost:9092");

        KStream<String, String> orders = b.stream("orders.raw");     // key=orderId, value="customerId,total"

        orders
            .selectKey((orderId, v) -> v.split(",")[0])                 // re-key -> repartition topic
            .groupByKey()
            .windowedBy(TimeWindows.ofSizeAndGrace(Duration.ofMinutes(5), Duration.ofMinutes(1)))
            .count(Materialized.as("orders-per-customer-store"))       // RocksDB + changelog topic
            .toStream()
            .map((win, count) -> KeyValue.pair(
                    win.key() + "@" + win.window().startTime(), String.valueOf(count)))
            .to("orders.per-customer.5m", Produced.with(Serdes.String(), Serdes.String()));

        KafkaStreams app = new KafkaStreams(b.build(), p);
        Runtime.getRuntime().addShutdownHook(new Thread(app::close));
        app.start();
    }
}

Note what the library does for you: the selectKey creates an internal repartition topic so all orders of a customer meet in one task; the window store is backed by a compacted changelog topic; exactly-once v2 wraps each task's output, store changelog writes and offsets in transactions.

8. Applications: How Teams Use Kafka and Queues

  • Microservice integration backbone. Domain events (OrderPlaced, PaymentCaptured) published via outbox; each downstream service owns a consumer group; ECST topics let services keep local read models instead of synchronous lookups.
  • Change data capture. Debezium streams database changes into Kafka for search indexing, cache invalidation (Chapter 3), data-lake ingestion and microservice extraction (strangler pattern: the new service is fed by CDC from the monolith's tables).
  • Activity and telemetry pipelines. Clickstream, logs and metrics are high-volume, loss-tolerant-ish and multi-consumer: the original LinkedIn use case. Producers use acks=1 or all depending on value, large batches and compression.
  • Stream processing. Fraud detection, real-time pricing, anomaly detection and real-time feature computation with Kafka Streams or Flink; results land in topics and online stores.
  • ML and AI systems. Real-time features for online inference; streaming document updates into embedding workers that upsert into vector indexes for RAG; logging prompts, responses and user feedback to topics for evaluation and fine-tuning datasets; buffering inference requests to GPU workers (where share groups' queue semantics fit well).
  • Task queues. Background jobs with retries, delays and priorities (thumbnail generation, emails) are often better on RabbitMQ or SQS; with share groups, Kafka is becoming viable when you already run it.
  • Event sourcing distribution. An event-sourced core writes to its event store, and a relay publishes to Kafka so other contexts can project the events.

9. Case Studies

9.1 LinkedIn: the origin of Kafka Verified

Kafka was built at LinkedIn to move activity events and operational metrics into both online systems and Hadoop, a job that existing message brokers handled poorly at that volume. The 2011 NetDB paper describes the design choices this chapter dissected: a pull-based consumer, partitioned append-only logs, reliance on the OS page cache and sendfile, and consumer-maintained offsets. Jay Kreps's essay "The Log" generalised the idea into the log as the integration backbone of a company. Sources: Kreps, Narkhede, Rao, "Kafka: a Distributed Messaging System for Log Processing" (NetDB workshop, 2011); "The Log" (LinkedIn Engineering, 2013).

9.2 Uber: retry topics and dead letter queues Verified

Uber's insurance engineering team described (2018) why simple in-place retries clog real-time processing and how they built a chain of delayed retry topics ending in a DLQ, with tooling to list, purge and merge (replay) DLQ messages back into the first retry topic so they never impede live traffic. Spring Kafka's @RetryableTopic implements essentially this design. Source: Uber Engineering, "Building Reliable Reprocessing and Dead Letter Queues with Apache Kafka".

9.3 The New York Times: the log as source of truth Verified

The Times described (2017) a publishing architecture in which every piece of published content is appended to a Kafka topic retained indefinitely, and downstream systems (search, personalisation, APIs) are materialised views built by consuming that log, so a new view or a rebuilt one is a replay from the beginning. It is a clear production example of event-carried state transfer and log-as-database thinking. Source: Confluent blog, "Publishing with Apache Kafka at The New York Times".

9.4 Exactly-once semantics in Kafka Verified

When Kafka 0.11 introduced idempotent producers and transactions, Confluent's engineers published a detailed explanation of the design (PIDs and sequence numbers, transaction coordinator, markers, zombie fencing) and its scope: exactly-once processing for Kafka-to-Kafka pipelines, not magical end-to-end delivery. The design document KIP-98 is the primary reference. Sources: Confluent, "Exactly-once Semantics are Possible: Here's How Apache Kafka Does it"; KIP-98.

9.5 Removing ZooKeeper: KIP-500 to Kafka 4.0 Verified

KIP-500 (2019) argued that maintaining metadata in an external ZooKeeper ensemble limited scalability (controller failover had to reload all metadata) and doubled operational burden. The replacement, KRaft, became production-ready for new clusters in 3.3 and the only mode in 4.0 (March 2025). Lesson for your own designs: putting metadata in a replicated log that consumers tail incrementally beats "reload the world" failover. Sources: KIP-500; Apache Kafka 4.0.0 release announcement.

12. Common Mistakes and Production Failures

  1. Assuming global ordering. Order exists only per partition. Two events for the same order with different keys (or null keys) can be consumed out of order.
  2. Increasing partitions on a keyed topic in place. Keys re-map, so for a while events of one entity are spread across two partitions and consumed out of order, and Kafka Streams state is sharded wrongly. Plan partitions up front or migrate to a new topic.
  3. Processing in a thread pool with auto-commit. Offsets are committed before work finishes; a crash loses records. Commit only after processing, or use Spring's container concurrency instead of your own executor.
  4. Slow processing exceeding max.poll.interval.ms. The consumer is kicked out, its partitions are reassigned, the same batch is redelivered to someone else, who is also slow: a rebalance loop. Reduce max.poll.records, speed up processing, or move slow work off the poll thread with explicit pause/resume.
  5. Retrying poison pills forever. A single bad record stops a partition for hours. Use ErrorHandlingDeserializer, classify exceptions and dead-letter.
  6. acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1. When the ISR shrinks to the leader alone, acknowledged writes exist on one disk. Set 2 with RF 3.
  7. Enabling unclean leader election to "fix" offline partitions without understanding it discards acknowledged data.
  8. Treating the DLT as a trash can. No alert, no replay tool, no owner: the DLT becomes silent data loss.
  9. Breaking schema compatibility. Renaming a field or changing its type in a shared topic breaks consumers that deploy later. Enforce compatibility in the registry and in CI.
  10. Huge messages. Multi-MB records strain broker memory and replication. Use the claim-check pattern: store the blob in object storage and send a reference.
  11. One consumer group per pod. Using a random group.id per instance makes every instance read every record (broadcast) instead of sharing load. Sometimes intended (cache invalidation), usually a bug.
  12. Assuming exactly-once covers your database. Kafka transactions do not include JDBC writes. Use the idempotent consumer pattern.
  13. Hot partitions. A skewed key distribution puts most traffic on one partition and one consumer. Monitor per-partition bytes-in and lag, and salt or split hot keys where per-key order permits.

Production failure pattern

Illustrative A deploy of 30 consumer pods under the eager protocol triggers 30 successive rebalances; each pauses the whole group for the rebalance timeout. Lag climbs for 20 minutes after every release. Fix: cooperative-sticky or KIP-848, static membership with group.instance.id = pod name, and a rolling-update pace slower than the session timeout.

13.2 Sizing and capacity

  • Plan from ingress MB/s, retention, RF, number of consumer groups and peak-to-average ratio (section 6.4). Leave 30–50% headroom on disk and network.
  • Use at least three brokers across three AZs (rack awareness via broker.rack) so RF=3 replicas land in different AZs.
  • Memory: brokers need modest heap (a few GB) and lots of free RAM for page cache, so most consumers read from memory.
  • Use tiered storage for long retention instead of huge broker disks.
  • Rebalance partition leadership and replica placement after adding brokers (Cruise Control or vendor tooling); new brokers do not take existing partitions automatically.

13.3 Security

  • Encrypt in transit (TLS) and authenticate clients (SASL/SCRAM, mTLS, or OAUTHBEARER/OIDC).
  • Authorise with ACLs per principal: producers write only their topics, consumer groups read only theirs; deny by default.
  • Treat topics containing PII as regulated data stores: retention aligned with policy, field-level encryption for sensitive attributes, and tombstone-based deletion for compacted topics to support erasure requests.
  • Schema registry and Connect REST APIs need authentication too; an open Connect API can run arbitrary connectors.

13.4 Reliability practices

  • Durable defaults: RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all, idempotent producers, unclean election disabled.
  • Graceful shutdown: Spring's container stops polling, finishes in-flight records and commits; Kubernetes terminationGracePeriodSeconds must exceed your processing time.
  • Disaster recovery across regions with MirrorMaker 2 or vendor cluster linking; remember offsets differ between clusters and must be translated (MirrorMaker 2 checkpoints). Chapter 9 covers multi-region patterns.
  • Test failure modes: kill brokers, partition the network, and verify lag recovery and zero data loss in staging.

Exercise 1 · Foundational

A topic has 12 partitions. Group A has 4 consumers, group B has 15. How many partitions does each consumer in each group get, and what happens to the extra consumers? What changes if a 13th partition is added?

Solution

Group A: 3 partitions each. Group B: 12 consumers get one partition each and 3 are idle (hot standbys that take over on failure). Groups are independent, so both read all 12 partitions. Adding a 13th partition: group A gets a 4/3/3/3 split, one of B's idle consumers activates; keyed data now maps differently, so per-key order is disrupted across the transition.

Exercise 2 · Intermediate

With RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all: describe what producers and consumers experience when (a) one broker fails, (b) a second broker fails, (c) both return. Repeat for acks=1.

Solution

(a) Affected partitions elect a new leader from the ISR (brief produce errors retried automatically); ISR size 2 satisfies min ISR, so writes and reads continue with no loss. (b) Partitions that had replicas on both failed brokers now have ISR size 1: producers get NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS and retry until delivery.timeout.ms; consumers can still read committed data from the surviving leader. If the failed brokers held the only ISR members, the partition is offline (unclean election disabled). (c) Brokers catch up, rejoin the ISR, writes resume. With acks=1, writes continue in (b), but a record acknowledged by a leader that then dies before followers copy it is lost.

Exercise 3 · Intermediate

A Spring listener calls a slow external fraud API (p99 4 s). With max.poll.records=500 and default settings the group rebalances constantly. Diagnose and propose three fixes.

Solution

500 records × up to 4 s can exceed max.poll.interval.ms (300 s), so the consumer is evicted and the batch is redelivered elsewhere. Fixes: (1) reduce max.poll.records (e.g., 50) so a batch finishes in time; (2) increase partitions and concurrency so each consumer handles fewer records; (3) process asynchronously with bounded parallelism per partition key (e.g., Confluent Parallel Consumer, or pause partitions while a batch is in flight and resume after), committing only contiguous completed offsets; plus timeouts and a circuit breaker on the fraud call (Chapter 8) so failures go to retry topics quickly.

Exercise 4 · Advanced

Design a pipeline that consumes payments, computes a per-merchant running total, and writes it to merchant-totals with no double counting under crashes. Then extend it to also update a PostgreSQL reporting table correctly.

Solution

Kafka part: Kafka Streams with exactly_once_v2: re-key by merchantId, groupByKey().aggregate(...) into a state store, output to merchant-totals. Input offsets, changelog writes and output commit atomically. PostgreSQL part: a separate consumer of merchant-totals with read_committed performing an idempotent upsert: INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (merchant_id) DO UPDATE SET total = EXCLUDED.total WHERE reporting.version < EXCLUDED.version, where version is the source offset or a sequence emitted by the Streams app. Because the output is an absolute total (not a delta), replays are naturally idempotent. Alternatively use a JDBC sink connector in upsert mode keyed by merchant id.

15. Interview Questions

Conceptual How does Kafka differ from RabbitMQ?

Kafka is a partitioned, replicated log: consumers pull and track offsets, records are retained and replayable, ordering is per partition, many groups read the same data cheaply, and parallelism is bounded by partitions. RabbitMQ is a broker with exchanges routing to queues: messages are pushed and removed on ack, with per-message TTL, priority and flexible routing; competing consumers scale freely but replay requires streams. Choose by need for replay/fan-out/throughput versus routing/per-message semantics.

Conceptual What is the high watermark and why can't consumers read past it?

The HW is the highest offset replicated to all ISR members (minimum LEO across the ISR). Records above it exist only on some replicas and could disappear if the leader fails and a replica without them becomes leader. Exposing only committed records ensures consumers never observe data that is later rolled back.

Conceptual How does the idempotent producer prevent duplicates and reordering?

The broker assigns a producer id and epoch; every batch carries a per-partition sequence number. The leader keeps recent sequences per producer and drops batches it already appended (returning success) while rejecting out-of-order ones so the producer resends in order. This allows up to five in-flight requests without reordering. It covers retries within one producer session only.

Architecture Explain eager, cooperative and KIP-848 rebalancing and when each matters.

Eager: everyone revokes everything, rejoins, and gets a new assignment: a stop-the-world pause per membership change. Cooperative incremental (KIP-429): members keep partitions that do not move; only moved partitions pause, at the cost of an extra round. KIP-848 (GA in 4.0): the broker computes assignments and reconciles each member independently via heartbeats; no group-wide barrier, assignors run server-side. Matters for large groups, frequent deploys and autoscaling. Static membership avoids rebalances on quick restarts in all classic modes.

Scenario Consumer lag is growing steadily on one topic. Walk through your diagnosis.

Check whether lag is on all partitions (capacity: processing rate below produce rate) or a few (hot keys, a stuck consumer, poison pill). Check rebalance frequency (instability) and max.poll.interval evictions. Look at processing latency of downstream dependencies (DB, APIs) and error/DLT rates. Check broker fetch latency and whether consumers read old data from disk. Fixes: scale consumers up to the partition count, increase partitions (new topic if keyed), optimise processing (batching DB writes), fix the hot key or poison pill, or apply back-pressure upstream.

Scenario After a broker crash some acknowledged messages are missing. What configuration allowed it?

Likely acks=1 (leader acked before followers had the data), or acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1 while the ISR had shrunk to the leader, or unclean leader election enabled so an out-of-sync replica became leader. Fix: RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all, unclean election disabled, idempotent producer.

Coding Configure Spring Kafka so a deserialisation error does not block a partition.

Wrap key and value deserializers in ErrorHandlingDeserializer with delegate classes, and register a DefaultErrorHandler with a DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer, marking DeserializationException as not retryable. The failed bytes and exception headers go to the DLT, the offset advances, and processing continues (sections 7.1 and 7.3).

Architecture Event notification versus event-carried state transfer: which would you choose for a customer-address change consumed by 8 services?

Usually ECST on a compacted topic keyed by customer id: consumers keep local copies, avoid callback storms against the customer service and remain available when it is down; new consumers bootstrap from the compacted topic. Choose notification if the data is sensitive (PII minimisation), very large, or consumers need it rarely. Hybrid: thin event plus a versioned snapshot topic.

Conceptual What does log compaction guarantee and what does it not?

It guarantees that the latest value for each key is retained (tombstones delete keys after delete.retention.ms), offsets never change, and any consumer reading from the start sees at least the final state of every key. It does not guarantee that intermediate values are removed promptly or at all (active segment, dirty ratio, compaction lag), nor that you see every intermediate update unless you read within min.compaction.lag.ms.

Behavioral Describe a messaging incident you handled.

Structure: impact (lag, data loss, duplicates), detection (alerts, customers), diagnosis (metrics such as URP and lag, logs, group describe), mitigation (scale, skip poison pill, reset offsets carefully), root cause (config, code, capacity), and prevention (defaults, runbooks, DLT tooling, chaos tests).

16. Knowledge Check

  • Distinguish event notification, event-carried state transfer, event sourcing and CQRS; they have different schemas, retention and coupling.
  • Queues delete on ack and route flexibly; logs retain, replay and fan out cheaply, ordered per partition.
  • Producers batch per partition (batch.size, linger.ms), compress whole batches, and with the idempotent producer retry without duplicates or reordering.
  • Segments plus sparse memory-mapped indexes make any offset cheap to find; retention and compaction work on closed segments.
  • Durability = RF 3 + min.insync.replicas=2 + acks=all + no unclean election. Consumers read only up to the high watermark (LSO for read_committed).
  • Rebalancing evolved from eager to cooperative to broker-driven KIP-848; static membership avoids restart rebalances; share groups add queue semantics.
  • Kafka transactions give exactly-once within Kafka; external effects still need idempotency. Poison pills need DLTs with tooling; schemas need a registry and compatibility rules.

A topic has 8 partitions and a consumer group has 10 members. What happens?

  1. Each member receives 0.8 partitions on average via shared fetching
  2. The group fails to start
  3. 8 members get one partition each and 2 are idle (classic groups)
  4. Kafka adds 2 partitions automatically
In consumer groups a partition is owned by one member, so parallelism caps at the partition count. Share groups (KIP-932) are the mechanism that lets several consumers share a partition.

What does isolation.level=read_committed change for a consumer?

  1. It reads only up to the last stable offset and skips records from aborted transactions
  2. It commits offsets transactionally
  3. It reads only from in-sync replicas
  4. It enables exactly-once for database writes
The consumer stops at the LSO (first open transaction) and filters aborted records. Offset commits and external writes are separate concerns.

Why did per-key ordering break after the team increased partitions from 12 to 24?

  1. The idempotent producer was disabled by the change
  2. Compaction reordered the records
  3. Consumers switched to read_uncommitted
  4. hash(key) mod partitions maps many keys to new partitions, so old and new events of a key sit in different partitions
Partition choice depends on the partition count; old events stay in the old partition while new ones go elsewhere, and the two partitions are consumed independently.

Which statement about KIP-848 is correct (as of Kafka 4.x)?

  1. It removes consumer groups in favour of share groups
  2. The group coordinator computes assignments and reconciles members incrementally via heartbeats, enabled with group.protocol=consumer
  3. It requires ZooKeeper
  4. It makes eager rebalancing the default
KIP-848 moves assignment to the broker and eliminates the group-wide synchronisation barrier; it is opt-in in 4.x with the classic protocol still default.

A downstream team needs every customer's current address on startup without calling the customer service. Best design?

  1. Thin notification events with a REST callback
  2. A delete-policy topic with 7-day retention
  3. A compacted topic keyed by customer id carrying the full address (event-carried state transfer)
  4. A RabbitMQ fanout exchange
Compaction retains the latest state per key indefinitely, so a new consumer reading from the beginning bootstraps the full dataset.
