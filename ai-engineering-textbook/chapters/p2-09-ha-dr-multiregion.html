Observability, High Availability, Disaster Recovery & Multi-Region

Previous chapters taught you to build services that behave correctly when individual calls fail. This chapter zooms out to the failure of whole machines, availability zones and regions, and to the question every on-call engineer faces first: what is actually happening? It covers observability for distributed systems, the design principles of high availability (redundancy, health checks, active-active, cells, static stability), disaster recovery strategies and how to prove they work, multi-region architecture with its data-replication and routing trade-offs, and the cloud-native principles that make all of this operable.

  • Prerequisites: Chapter 1 (SLOs, estimation), Chapter 5 (replication), Chapter 6 (consistency, CAP), Chapter 8 (resilience patterns).
  • You will be able to: instrument a Spring Boot service with metrics, traces, logs and correlation IDs; choose RED, USE or golden signals for a component; design health checks that help rather than hurt; remove single points of failure; compare active-passive, active-active and cell-based designs; apply static stability; pick a DR strategy from RPO/RTO and cost; run DR tests and chaos experiments; design multi-region data replication, global routing and failover runbooks; and apply 12-factor and immutable-infrastructure principles.

1. Introduction: Designing for the Day the Data Centre Disappears

High availability is not a feature you add; it is a property that emerges from many decisions: how many copies of each component exist, where they run, how failures are detected, how traffic moves away from failures, and whether the remaining capacity can absorb the load. Disaster recovery is the plan for when those decisions are not enough: a region fails, data is corrupted, or an operator deletes the production database. Both depend on observability, the ability to ask arbitrary questions about the system's behaviour from its outputs, because you cannot fail over from, or recover from, a failure you have not detected and understood.

For a Spring Boot engineer the building blocks are familiar: Actuator health endpoints, Micrometer metrics, logs with MDC, database replicas, Kubernetes deployments with several replicas. What changes at this level is the scope (zones and regions rather than pods), the quantitative reasoning (RPO, RTO, error budgets, blast radius), and the discipline (runbooks, game days, chaos experiments) needed to make recovery routine rather than heroic.

AI-era systems raise the stakes in specific ways. GPU capacity is scarce and often concentrated in particular regions, so "fail over to another region" may mean "fail over to a region with half the inference capacity". Vector indexes and feature stores are large derived datasets whose rebuild time determines RTO. Model providers are external dependencies with their own regional outages. And observability must extend to quality signals (hallucination rate, retrieval hit rate, token spend) alongside latency and errors.

Bridging from Java & Spring Boot

What you already know
Spring Boot Actuator (/actuator/health, /actuator/prometheus), Micrometer meters and timers, SLF4J/Logback with MDC, Spring Cloud Sleuth or Micrometer Tracing, HikariCP pool metrics, database read replicas, and deploying several replicas of a service behind a load balancer.
Why that is not enough
Three replicas in one availability zone are one failure domain. A health check that pings the database can take down every instance during a database blip. Backups that have never been restored are hopes, not backups. And a dashboard of CPU graphs does not tell you whether users can check out.
The modern approach
Instrument with OpenTelemetry-compatible signals (metrics, traces, logs, increasingly profiles) correlated by trace id; alert on SLO burn rates of user-facing signals; spread every tier across at least three zones; design for static stability so the system survives a zone loss without control-plane actions; isolate blast radius with cells; choose a DR strategy per workload from its RPO/RTO; and continuously verify with restore tests, game days and chaos experiments.
Where Python and Go fit
OpenTelemetry SDKs exist for both, with the same semantic conventions, so traces flow across Java, Go and Python services. Go is the language of most cloud-native infrastructure you will operate (Kubernetes, Prometheus, etcd, the OpenTelemetry Collector); Python dominates ops scripting and ML pipelines whose recovery you must also plan.
In production
Prometheus or a managed metrics service, Grafana, an OpenTelemetry Collector pipeline to a tracing backend (Tempo, Jaeger, vendor APM), structured JSON logs to Loki/Elasticsearch, multi-AZ Kubernetes and managed databases, cross-region replicas and backups, DNS or anycast-based global routing, and runbooks exercised in regular game days.
Use it when / avoid it when
Multi-AZ is the baseline for any production workload. Multi-region active-active is justified only when the business impact of a regional outage or global latency exceeds its considerable cost and complexity; many systems are better served by multi-AZ plus a tested warm-standby or pilot-light DR region.

This chapter treats observability at the level of system design: what to measure and why. The tooling (Prometheus internals, Grafana, OpenTelemetry Collector configuration, log pipelines) is covered in depth in Chapter 12.5.

3. Fundamentals

3.1 Monitoring versus observability

Monitoring answers known questions: is CPU above 80%, is the error rate above 1%? Observability is the property that lets you answer new questions without shipping new code: why are checkout requests from Android clients in eu-west slow only for customers with more than 50 cart items? That requires high-cardinality, correlated telemetry: you must be able to slice by arbitrary attributes and jump from an aggregate (a latency spike) to individual examples (traces) to the detail (logs, profiles).

3.2 The pillars: metrics, traces, logs, and profiles

  • Metrics: numeric time series aggregated over time (counters, gauges, histograms). Cheap to store and query, ideal for alerting and dashboards, but they lose per-request detail and their cost grows with label cardinality (a label with user ids creates a series per user).
  • Traces: the causal tree of spans for a single request across services, each with timing and attributes. They show where time goes and which dependency failed. Volume forces sampling (head-based: decide at the start; tail-based: decide after seeing the whole trace, e.g., keep all errors and slow traces).
  • Logs: timestamped discrete events, ideally structured (JSON) with a trace id and span id. Rich context, expensive at volume.
  • Profiles: continuous sampling of where CPU time, allocations or lock contention go, down to the function. They answer "why is this service slow or expensive" when traces only say "this span took 300 ms". Async-profiler and JFR on the JVM, pprof in Go, and continuous-profiling backends (Pyroscope, Parca, vendor tools) provide this.

The value comes from correlation: exemplars link a metric data point to a trace; logs carry the trace id; profiles can be linked to spans. The OpenTelemetry project standardises APIs, SDKs, the wire protocol (OTLP), the Collector, and semantic conventions (standard attribute names like http.request.method) across all of these.

3.3 What to measure: golden signals, RED and USE

MethodSignalsApply to
Four golden signals (Google SRE)Latency (split successful vs failed), traffic, errors, saturationAny user-facing system
RED (Tom Wilkie)Rate, Errors, Duration per endpoint or operationRequest-driven services: every microservice, every dependency call
USE (Brendan Gregg)Utilisation, Saturation, Errors per resourceResources: CPUs, disks, network interfaces, connection pools, thread pools, queues, GPUs

Use RED for services and USE for the resources beneath them; the golden signals are the union for a user journey. Saturation is the most neglected and most predictive: queue depth, pool wait time, CPU run-queue length, and GC pause time tell you a failure is coming before latency spikes. For AI services add token throughput, time to first token, cost per request, cache hit rates and quality metrics.

3.4 SLIs, SLOs and alerting on burn rate

Chapter 1 introduced SLOs. For HA the key practice is alerting on error-budget burn rate rather than on raw thresholds: with a 99.9% monthly SLO, a burn rate of 14.4 over one hour consumes 2% of the monthly budget in that hour, which is worth paging for; a slow burn of 1–3 over days deserves a ticket. Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts (fast and slow windows) catch both sudden outages and gradual degradations with few false positives, as described in Google's SRE workbook.

3.5 Correlation IDs and context propagation

A correlation id ties together every log line, span and message produced by one logical operation across services. The modern standard is the W3C Trace Context traceparent header (00-<trace-id>-<span-id>-<flags>) plus tracestate, and baggage for application key-values. Micrometer Tracing and OpenTelemetry instrumentation propagate it automatically over HTTP clients, servlet and WebFlux servers, Kafka headers and many other transports, and put traceId/spanId into the logging MDC. Asynchronous boundaries (thread pools, @Async, reactive operators, message queues) are where propagation breaks; use context-propagating executors and instrumented clients.

3.6 Availability, failure domains and single points of failure

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) a service performs correctly. A failure domain is a set of components that can fail together: a process, a host, a rack, an availability zone (one or more data centres with independent power and networking), a region, a cloud provider, and also logical domains like a deployment, a configuration push or a shared dependency. A single point of failure (SPOF) is any component whose failure makes the service unavailable. Classic SPOFs that survive "we run three replicas": a single NAT gateway, a single primary database without automated failover, a DNS provider, a certificate that expires, a secrets manager in one region, a CI/CD system needed to deploy a fix, and humans (the one engineer who knows how to fail over).

3.7 Redundancy models

  • N+1 / N+2: enough capacity to lose one (or two) units. Across three zones, to survive a zone loss each zone must be able to carry 50% of peak load, i.e., provision 150% of peak in total.
  • Active-passive (hot, warm, cold standby): a standby takes over when the active fails. Simple data consistency (one writer); failover takes time and the standby may not work when needed if not exercised.
  • Active-active: all sites serve traffic. Capacity is used, failover is just "stop sending traffic there", and the passive-rot problem disappears, but data written in multiple places must be replicated and reconciled (Chapter 6).

3.8 Health checks

Health checks drive automated recovery, so badly designed ones cause outages. Kubernetes distinguishes three probes:

  • Liveness: "is this process stuck beyond repair?" Failure restarts the container. It should check only the process itself (event loop responsive, not deadlocked), never downstream dependencies.
  • Readiness: "should this instance receive traffic now?" Failure removes it from Service endpoints without restarting. Use it during startup, warm-up, graceful shutdown and local overload. Checking a hard dependency here is debatable: if the database is down for everyone, removing every pod from rotation turns degraded service into no service.
  • Startup: protects slow-starting apps from liveness kills during boot.

Spring Boot Actuator exposes /actuator/health/liveness and /actuator/health/readiness groups backed by ApplicationAvailability, and by default does not include external dependencies in the liveness group. Load balancers have their own health checks (shallow vs deep); deep checks across a fleet must fail open: if more than a threshold of targets look unhealthy, keep routing to all of them (AWS ALB and Route 53 behave this way in some configurations), because a fleet-wide health failure is more likely a bad check than a fleet-wide outage.

3.9 Cell-based architecture

A cell is a complete, independent instance of a workload (its services, data stores and queues) that serves a subset of customers, selected by a partition key such as tenant id. A thin cell router maps each request to its cell. Cells do not call each other or share data stores. Benefits: a bad deployment, poison request, noisy tenant or data corruption affects one cell's customers, not everyone; cells scale out by adding more cells of a tested size; and deployments roll out cell by cell. Costs: the router becomes critical (keep it extremely simple and statically stable), cross-cell features (global search, analytics) need separate aggregation paths, migrating tenants between cells is non-trivial, and fixed per-cell overhead. Shuffle sharding is a related technique: give each customer a random small subset of resources so that any two customers rarely share all their resources, limiting the blast radius of a poison customer.

3.10 Static stability

A system is statically stable if it keeps working through a failure without needing to make changes, especially without calling a control plane (an API that creates, scales or reconfigures resources). The motivating observation from AWS: control planes are more complex and less available than data planes, and during a large event everyone calls them at once. Examples: pre-provision capacity in each zone to absorb a zone loss instead of relying on autoscaling to launch instances during the event; cache credentials and configuration so instances keep running if the config service is down; keep the last known good routing table if the discovery service fails. Static stability trades extra cost (idle headroom) for not depending on anything during recovery.

3.11 Disaster recovery: RPO and RTO

  • Recovery Point Objective (RPO): maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time. RPO 5 min means you may lose at most the last 5 minutes of writes.
  • Recovery Time Objective (RTO): maximum acceptable time from disaster to restored service.

Disasters include region loss, but far more often logical events: a bad migration, ransomware, a buggy batch job that corrupts data, an accidental DROP TABLE. Replication does not protect against logical corruption, because the corruption replicates too. Point-in-time recovery from backups and immutable (write-once) backup copies in a separate account are the defence.

3.13 DR testing and chaos engineering

A DR plan that has not been executed recently does not work; configuration drift, expired credentials, missing capacity quotas and unknown dependencies accumulate silently. Testing levels: tabletop exercises (walk through the runbook), component tests (restore a backup into a scratch environment and verify data), game days (simulate a zone or region failure in staging or production with the team), and full failover drills. Chaos engineering generalises this into a practice: form a hypothesis about steady-state behaviour ("checkout success rate stays above 99.5% if one zone's instances are terminated"), inject a real-world fault (instance termination, latency, dependency failure, zone evacuation), observe, and minimise blast radius while you learn. Netflix's Chaos Monkey (instance termination) and Chaos Kong (region evacuation) are the canonical examples; managed services such as AWS Fault Injection Service and open-source tools such as Chaos Mesh and LitmusChaos bring the practice to Kubernetes.

3.14 Multi-region architecture

Reasons to go multi-region: survive regional outages, serve users with lower latency, and meet data residency requirements (some data must stay in a jurisdiction). Each reason implies a different design. The core problems are:

  • Data replication strategy: single-writer with async cross-region replicas (simple; failover loses replication lag worth of data; reads in remote regions are stale), single-writer per partition with home regions (each user's data has a home; writes routed there; avoids conflicts), multi-writer with conflict resolution (DynamoDB global tables-style LWW or CRDTs), or synchronous consensus across regions (Spanner, CockroachDB multi-region; RPO zero, but every write pays inter-region latency).
  • Global traffic routing: DNS-based (GeoDNS, latency-based, weighted, failover records with health checks; limited by resolver caching and TTLs), anycast (the same IP announced from many locations via BGP; the network routes users to the nearest edge; failover by withdrawing routes, typically seconds; used by CDNs, Cloudflare, Google Cloud's global load balancer and AWS Global Accelerator), and application-level routing (the client or edge picks a region from a user's home-region mapping).
  • Latency: the speed of light in fibre is about 200 km/ms, so a transatlantic round trip is at least ~60–80 ms in practice. Chatty cross-region calls are unacceptable on the request path.
  • Conflict handling: any multi-writer design needs the conflict-resolution machinery of Chapter 6 (LWW, vector clocks, CRDTs, or home-region routing to avoid conflicts).
  • Failover runbooks: who decides, with which signals, which steps (fence the old primary, promote replicas, shift traffic, scale up, verify), and how to fail back.

3.15 Cloud-native design principles

  • Twelve-factor: one codebase, explicit dependencies, config in the environment, backing services as attached resources, strict build/release/run separation, stateless processes, port binding, concurrency via processes, disposability (fast start, graceful stop), dev/prod parity, logs as event streams, admin processes as one-off jobs. Spring Boot's externalised configuration, Actuator and fat-jar/container model map naturally to these.
  • Immutable infrastructure: never patch running servers; build a new image (container or VM) and replace. Combined with infrastructure as code (Terraform, Pulumi, CloudFormation), it makes environments reproducible, which is the foundation of DR: you can recreate a region from code.
  • Autoscaling: horizontal pod autoscaling on CPU or custom metrics (queue lag via KEDA), node autoscaling (Cluster Autoscaler, Karpenter), and scheduled scaling for predictable peaks. Autoscaling handles gradual load changes; it is not an HA mechanism for sudden zone loss (static stability).
  • Managed services trade-offs: managed databases, queues and caches buy you multi-AZ replication, backups and patching, at the cost of less control, provider-specific failure modes and limits, and potential lock-in. You inherit their SLAs (and must read what they exclude), and you still own the configuration that makes them highly available (multi-AZ flags, backup retention, cross-region copies).

4. Internal Working

4.1 How a trace is built and propagated

  1. An inbound request reaches the Spring MVC filter chain. The tracing filter (Micrometer Observation with an OpenTelemetry or Brave bridge) looks for a traceparent header. If present, it continues that trace; if absent, it creates a new 128-bit trace id and makes a sampling decision.
  2. A server span is started with attributes (HTTP method, route template, status). The current span is stored in a context (thread-local, or Reactor context in WebFlux), and trace and span ids are copied into the logging MDC.
  3. Instrumented outbound clients (RestClient, WebClient, JDBC via datasource-micrometer, KafkaTemplate with observation enabled) start client spans as children and inject traceparent into outgoing headers (HTTP headers, Kafka record headers).
  4. On completion each span is ended and handed to an exporter, which batches spans and sends them via OTLP to an OpenTelemetry Collector.
  5. The Collector applies processors (batching, attribute redaction, tail sampling that keeps all errors and slow traces), then exports to the tracing backend. Metrics may be derived from spans (span-metrics) and exemplars link histogram buckets to trace ids.

Sampling must be consistent: if service A samples a trace, downstream services must honour the sampled flag in traceparent, or traces become fragmented. Tail sampling requires all spans of a trace to reach the same Collector instance (load-balance by trace id).

4.2 How a metric becomes an alert

Micrometer records measurements into meters in memory (a Timer keeps counts, sums and, if configured, histogram buckets). Prometheus scrapes /actuator/prometheus every 15–60 s (pull model), or an OTLP exporter pushes. The time-series database stores samples per unique label set. Recording rules pre-compute aggregates (error ratio per service over 5 minutes); alerting rules evaluate expressions such as multi-window burn rates and send firing alerts to Alertmanager, which deduplicates, groups, silences and routes them to on-call. Latency must be aggregated from histograms, not averaged percentiles: percentiles from different instances cannot be averaged, but histogram buckets can be summed and the quantile computed afterwards.

4.3 Failure detection and failover inside a region

  • Instance or pod failure: the kubelet's liveness probe restarts a hung container; a node failure is detected by missing node heartbeats (node lease), pods are evicted after a toleration timeout (default 5 minutes for unreachable nodes, often tuned down), and the scheduler places replacements. Meanwhile, readiness and endpoint updates stop traffic to pods on the failed node.
  • Load balancer: health checks against targets at an interval with healthy/unhealthy thresholds; failed targets are removed; connection draining (deregistration delay) lets in-flight requests finish during planned removal.
  • Database primary failure: a managed service (e.g., RDS Multi-AZ, Cloud SQL HA) detects primary failure, promotes the synchronous standby in another zone and updates a DNS name, typically within a minute or two; clients must reconnect (connection pools need validation and short DNS caching; the JVM's DNS cache TTL matters). Self-managed PostgreSQL uses Patroni with an etcd/Consul leader key and fencing to avoid two primaries.
  • Zone failure: a statically stable design simply loses a third of capacity; load balancers stop sending to unhealthy targets in that zone; zonal autoscaling may add capacity elsewhere if the control plane is healthy, but the design must not depend on it. Gray failures (a zone partially degraded rather than down) are harder: health checks pass but latency rises. Zonal shift capabilities (e.g., Route 53 Application Recovery Controller zonal shift) let operators or automation evacuate a zone deliberately.

4.4 Cross-region database replication mechanics

  • Asynchronous physical/log shipping: the primary streams its write-ahead log or binlog to replicas in other regions (PostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL replication, Aurora Global Database's storage-level replication). Replication lag is the RPO on failover. Promotion: stop writes to the old primary (fence), promote a replica, repoint writers.
  • Logical / CDC replication: Debezium or native logical replication ships row changes; allows different schemas or engines on each side and bidirectional setups (with conflict handling).
  • Multi-active stores: DynamoDB global tables, Cassandra multi-DC, Cosmos DB multi-region writes: each region accepts writes and replicates asynchronously, resolving conflicts by last-writer-wins on timestamps (or configurable policies). Some providers now offer stronger multi-region consistency modes for selected tables at higher write latency; check the current documentation. Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026
  • Consensus across regions: Spanner, CockroachDB and YugabyteDB replicate each range with Raft or Paxos across regions; a write commits when a majority of replicas (in different regions) acknowledge. With a 3-region configuration, losing one region keeps the database fully consistent and available; each write pays the round trip to the nearest majority. Placement controls (leaseholder/leader pinning, regional-by-row tables) keep latency low for local data.
  • Object storage: cross-region replication of buckets (asynchronous, often with an optional replication-time SLA). Use it for backups, model artifacts and data-lake files.

4.5 How global routing fails over

DNS failover: a DNS provider runs health checks against regional endpoints from many vantage points; when a region fails its checks, the provider stops returning that region's address. The effective failover time is detection time (check interval × failure threshold) plus TTL plus resolver and client caching, which can exceed the TTL (some clients and JVMs cache longer; the JVM security property networkaddress.cache.ttl controls Java's cache). Use low TTLs (30–60 s) on failover records and verify client behaviour. Anycast: edges announce the same prefix; if a site fails, its BGP announcement is withdrawn and routers converge on other sites within seconds; long-lived TCP connections to the failed site break and must reconnect. Global load balancers (anycast front ends that proxy to regional backends) combine both: users connect to the nearest edge, and the edge chooses a healthy backend region per request.

4.6 Anatomy of a regional failover runbook

  1. Detect and declare: SLO burn alerts and synthetic probes from outside the region; an incident commander declares a regional failover using pre-agreed criteria (to avoid both hesitation and flapping).
  2. Fence the failed region: stop it from accepting writes (disable writers, revoke credentials, set the database read-only if reachable) so that it cannot diverge if it comes back partially.
  3. Promote data stores in the recovery region (replicas to primaries), recording the replication position to quantify data loss.
  4. Scale up compute in the recovery region to full capacity (pre-provisioned if statically stable; otherwise this step dominates RTO and depends on capacity availability).
  5. Shift traffic (DNS weights, global load balancer configuration, or routing-control switches that were pre-created so the data plane needs no new resources).
  6. Verify with synthetic transactions and SLO dashboards; communicate status.
  7. Reconcile after recovery: writes that reached the old region but not the replica must be identified (from its WAL or audit logs) and replayed or compensated.
  8. Fail back deliberately later, usually by re-establishing replication in reverse and repeating a controlled switchover.

4.7 Cell routing internals

The cell router maps a partition key to a cell using a mapping table (tenant → cell) cached at the router, with consistent hashing or explicit assignment for placement. To stay statically stable the router serves from its cached mapping if the mapping store is unavailable, and new tenants are placed by an offline control plane. Migrating a tenant between cells follows a careful sequence: copy data, catch up with CDC, briefly block writes for that tenant, flip the mapping, resume. Deployments proceed in waves: one canary cell, then increasingly larger groups, with automated rollback on SLO regression, so a bad change hits a small fraction of customers.

6. Math: Availability, Error Budgets, RPO/RTO and Capacity

6.1 Availability and downtime

Allowed downtime per 30-day month for availability \( A \) is \( (1 - A) \times 43{,}200 \) minutes: 99.9% → 43.2 min, 99.95% → 21.6 min, 99.99% → 4.3 min. Annual: 99.99% ≈ 52.6 minutes per year. Every additional nine needs roughly an order of magnitude better detection, failover automation and change safety.

6.2 Redundancy math

With independent components of availability \( a \), \( n \) redundant copies where any one suffices give \( A = 1 - (1-a)^{n} \). Two zones at 99.9% → 99.9999% in theory. Reality is dominated by correlated failures (shared dependencies, bad deployments, control-plane events) and by failover imperfection. A useful refinement: if failover succeeds with probability \( p_f \), \( A \approx 1 - (1-a)\,[(1-a) + (1-p_f)] \). With \( a = 0.999 \) and \( p_f = 0.99 \): \( 1 - 0.001 \times (0.001 + 0.01) = 0.999989 \): the failover success rate, not the second copy, limits availability. Illustrative

6.3 Mean time to recovery

Steady-state availability \( A = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR} \). Halving MTTR has the same effect as doubling MTBF, and MTTR is usually far cheaper to improve (detection, automation, rehearsed runbooks). MTTR decomposes into time to detect + time to decide + time to mitigate; observability attacks the first two.

6.4 Error-budget burn rate

With SLO target \( S \), the error budget is \( 1-S \). Burn rate over a window is \( b = \frac{\text{observed error ratio}}{1 - S} \). A burn rate \( b \) sustained for time \( t \) consumes \( b \cdot t / T \) of a budget for period \( T \). For \( S = 99.9\% \), \( T = 30 \) days: an error ratio of 1.44% gives \( b = 14.4 \); in one hour it consumes \( 14.4 \times 1/720 = 2\% \) of the budget, the classic fast-burn paging threshold. A 6-hour window at \( b = 6 \) consumes 5%.

6.5 Static-stability headroom

To survive losing one of \( z \) zones without scaling, each zone must be able to carry \( \frac{1}{z-1} \) of peak load, so total provisioned capacity is \( \frac{z}{z-1} \) of peak: 150% for 3 zones, 133% for 4. For active-active across \( r \) regions surviving one region's loss: \( \frac{r}{r-1} \), i.e., 200% for two regions, which is why two-region active-active is expensive and three regions are often more economical.

6.6 RPO under asynchronous replication

If replication lag is \( L(t) \), the data lost on an unplanned failover at time \( t \) is roughly the writes in \( [t - L(t), t] \). RPO is therefore bounded by the maximum lag you tolerate: alert when \( L \) exceeds the RPO and consider throttling writes. Example: 2,000 writes/s with a p99 lag of 800 ms risks losing ~1,600 writes on failover, which must be reconciled from the old region's log or accepted. Illustrative

6.7 Restore time

Backup-and-restore RTO is dominated by data volume over restore throughput: \( RTO \approx t_{provision} + \frac{V}{\text{throughput}} + t_{replay} + t_{verify} \). Restoring 8 TB at 500 MB/s takes about 4.4 hours before log replay and verification. This is why large datasets (including vector indexes and feature stores) push you toward pilot light or warm standby even when the business could tolerate a few hours of downtime. Illustrative

6.8 Cell blast radius

With \( c \) equally sized cells and a failure confined to one cell, the fraction of customers affected is \( 1/c \). With shuffle sharding, each customer is assigned \( k \) of \( n \) workers; the probability that another specific customer shares all \( k \) workers is \( 1/\binom{n}{k} \). For \( n = 8, k = 2 \): \( 1/28 \approx 3.6\% \); for \( n = 100, k = 5 \): about \( 1.3 \times 10^{-8} \).
