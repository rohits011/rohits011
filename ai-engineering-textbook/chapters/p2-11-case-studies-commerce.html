Case Studies II: E-Commerce, Payments & Video Streaming

Money and media: the two workloads where mistakes are most visible. An e-commerce platform must never sell stock it does not have, even when a hundred thousand people press "buy" in the same second. A payment system must never charge twice, never lose a cent, and prove it to auditors. A video platform must move petabytes efficiently enough that the bandwidth bill does not consume the business. This chapter designs all three end to end and implements their hardest components in Spring Boot, Go and Python.

  • Prerequisites: database selection and indexing, transactions, sagas, outbox and idempotency, Kafka, resilience patterns, and Chapter 2.10 for the design method.
  • You will be able to: design inventory reservation that survives flash sales; build an idempotent payment API backed by a double-entry ledger with reconciliation; reduce PCI DSS scope through tokenization; and design an upload-to-playback video pipeline with an adaptive bitrate ladder, CDN strategy and scalable view counting.

1. Introduction: Correctness Under Contention, and Bytes at Scale

The previous chapter dealt with systems where the worst failure is usually a delayed or duplicated message. In this chapter the failures cost money directly. Overselling a limited-edition product means cancelling orders and losing customer trust; double-charging a card means chargebacks, fees and possibly regulatory attention; an inefficient video encoding ladder can multiply your CDN bill. These are also the systems where your Spring Boot experience is most directly relevant — commerce and payments are classic Java territory — and where the "it worked in the monolith" instincts are most dangerous once the system is distributed.

  • E-commerce is a federation of very different workloads: a read-heavy, search-driven catalog; a write-heavy, per-user cart; a contention-heavy inventory; and a transaction-heavy checkout that coordinates inventory, pricing, payment and fulfilment. Flash sales concentrate all of that onto a handful of SKUs for a few minutes.
  • Payments are about exactness: idempotent APIs because clients retry, an append-only double-entry ledger because money must be conserved, reconciliation because external processors are the true source of settlement, and tokenization because storing card numbers puts your entire estate in PCI DSS scope.
  • Video streaming is about throughput and cost: resumable uploads of multi-gigabyte files, a parallel transcoding pipeline, an adaptive bitrate ladder that trades quality against bandwidth, and a CDN strategy that keeps origin traffic tiny. View counting adds a classic stream-aggregation problem.

Bridging from Java & Spring Boot

What you already know
You have written @Transactional order services over JPA, probably decremented stock with UPDATE ... SET qty = qty - 1, integrated a payment gateway SDK, maybe used Spring State Machine or an enum-based status column, and run @Scheduled batch jobs for settlement reports. You may have streamed files with StreamingResponseBody or uploaded them to S3 with the AWS SDK.
Why that is not enough
A single @Transactional cannot span the inventory service, the payment service provider and the order database. A naive stock decrement on one hot row serialises every buyer of the flash-sale SKU. A retried HTTP call to a PSP without an idempotency key is a double charge. A balance column updated in place cannot be audited. A Spring service that proxies video bytes will saturate its NIC long before its CPU.
The modern approach
Reservations with TTLs and conditional updates (or atomic Redis scripts) for inventory; sagas with compensations orchestrated through an outbox; idempotency keys persisted with request fingerprints; an immutable double-entry ledger with balances as derived projections; tokenization via a PSP's hosted fields; asynchronous DAG-based transcoding; pre-signed URLs so bytes never pass through your application servers; CDNs with origin shielding.
Where Python and Go fit
Go is strong for the high-throughput edges: view-count ingestion, upload gateways, segment packagers and CDN-adjacent services. Python dominates the media and data tooling (FFmpeg orchestration, quality metrics such as VMAF, recommendation models, fraud-model training). Java/Spring remains the natural home for checkout, orders, payments and ledger services, where transactional rigour and a mature ecosystem matter most.
In production
Expect PostgreSQL or MySQL (often sharded) for orders, payments and ledger; Elasticsearch/OpenSearch for search; Redis for carts and flash-sale counters; Kafka with CDC for events; object storage plus a CDN for media; and a workflow engine (Temporal, AWS Step Functions, or a home-grown orchestrator) for transcoding and long-running payment flows.
Use it when / avoid it when
Use these designs when scale or regulation demands them. A small shop should use a hosted commerce platform and a PSP's hosted checkout — buying PCI compliance and fraud tooling is almost always cheaper than building it. A company with a few hundred videos should use a managed video platform. Build when the differentiation or the unit economics justify it.

2. History: How These Systems Evolved

2.1 E-commerce: from the monolith to the cell

The first generation of online retail (mid-1990s) ran on monolithic application servers and a single relational database. As traffic grew, the database became the bottleneck, and large retailers decomposed into services. Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper is the landmark artefact from this period: it describes a highly available key-value store built so that the shopping cart would accept writes even during failures, deliberately choosing availability and later reconciliation (merging divergent cart versions) over strict consistency. That trade-off — "never refuse an add-to-cart" — still shapes cart design today.

The 2010s added two pressures. First, flash sales and drops (limited releases, Singles' Day in China, Black Friday/Cyber Monday) created traffic spikes of an order of magnitude above baseline for a few minutes, concentrated on few products. Shopify has written publicly about building queueing and throttling into its load-balancer tier for these "flash" events (§9). Second, multi-tenancy: commerce platforms hosting millions of merchants adopted cell or "pod" architectures, in which each shard of merchants runs on an isolated slice of infrastructure so one merchant's sale cannot take down everyone.

2.2 Payments: from card imprinters to APIs and tokens

Card payments run on a four-party model: cardholder, issuer (the cardholder's bank), merchant, and acquirer (the merchant's bank), connected by card networks. Online merchants historically integrated with acquirers or gateways through complex, batch-oriented interfaces. Two developments changed the engineering picture. The card brands created a common security standard, PCI DSS, in 2004 and formed the PCI Security Standards Council in 2006; version 4.0 was published in 2022 and v4.0.1 in 2024, and v3.2.1 was retired in 2024. In parallel, developer-focused payment service providers (PSPs) such as Stripe (launched publicly in 2011) offered simple REST APIs, client-side tokenization and hosted payment fields, so merchants could accept cards without card numbers ever touching their servers.

Regulation then reshaped flows: the EU's PSD2 introduced Strong Customer Authentication, which made 3-D Secure 2 challenges a normal step in European card payments around 2019–2021, and made payment flows inherently multi-step and asynchronous. The "payment intent" style of API — an object that moves through states such as requires_payment_method, requires_action, processing and succeeded — is the industry's answer to that complexity. Internally, large platforms (Uber, Airbnb and others) published how they rebuilt payment systems around idempotency and double-entry ledgers (§9).

2.3 Video: from progressive download to adaptive streaming

Early web video was either progressive download (fetch an MP4 over HTTP and start playing when enough has arrived) or proprietary streaming protocols such as RTMP with Flash. Two ideas made modern streaming possible. HTTP-based adaptive streaming splits the video into short segments encoded at several bitrates and lets the player switch between them as bandwidth changes; Apple introduced HTTP Live Streaming (HLS) in 2009 (later published as RFC 8216 in 2017), and MPEG standardised DASH in 2012. Because segments are ordinary HTTP objects, ordinary CDNs can cache them. Purpose-built CDNs followed: Netflix began building its own CDN, Open Connect, placing caching appliances inside ISP networks.

Encoding also became smarter. Netflix's 2015 "per-title encode optimization" replaced a one-size-fits-all bitrate ladder with one tuned to each title's complexity, and later work moved to per-shot optimisation. Codecs advanced from H.264/AVC (2003) to HEVC (2013), VP9 (2013) and AV1 (2018), each saving bandwidth at the price of more encoding compute and narrower device support. Storage and metadata scaled too: YouTube built Vitess (from 2010, open-sourced in 2012) to shard the MySQL databases behind its metadata.

3. Fundamentals: The Building Blocks Under All Three Designs

3.1 Inventory as a contended counter

Inventory looks like a number, but semantically it is a set of claims: on hand, reserved (held for carts or checkouts in progress), committed (sold, awaiting fulfilment) and available = on hand − reserved − committed. The engineering question is where you place the decrement. Decrementing at add-to-cart over-reserves (most carts are abandoned); decrementing at payment success risks overselling between "checkout started" and "paid". The standard compromise is a reservation with a TTL created when checkout starts, converted to a commitment on payment success, and released on payment failure or timeout. For contention, the update must be a conditional write (UPDATE ... SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE sku = :s AND on_hand - reserved - committed >= :q), never a read-then-write in application code.

3.2 State machines for long-lived business objects

Orders, payments and transcoding jobs are long-lived objects driven by external events. Model each as an explicit finite-state machine with allowed transitions enforced in the database (UPDATE ... WHERE id = ? AND status = 'AUTHORIZED'), not just in Java. This gives you idempotency for free (a duplicate "capture succeeded" event finds the payment already in CAPTURED and does nothing) and makes concurrency races visible as failed conditional updates.

3.3 Sagas, outbox and idempotency (recap)

Checkout spans inventory, payment and order services. A two-phase commit across them is impractical (PSPs do not participate in your XA transactions), so checkout is a saga: a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action (release reservation, void authorization, refund). Every step publishes its result through a transactional outbox so that "state changed" and "event published" cannot diverge, and every consumer is idempotent. All three mechanisms are developed in Chapter 2.6; here we apply them.

3.4 Double-entry bookkeeping for engineers

Double-entry accounting records every movement of value as at least two entries — debits and credits — that sum to zero. Each entry belongs to an account (a customer's balance, a merchant's payable, the platform's fee revenue, cash held at the PSP). Balances are not stored as mutable fields; they are the sum of entries, materialised for speed. The invariants are mechanical and therefore checkable: every transaction balances, entries are immutable (corrections are new reversing entries), and the sum over all accounts is zero. For engineers, the key insight is that a ledger is an event-sourced, append-only log with a conservation law, which makes it easy to audit, replay and reconcile.

3.5 Card payment lifecycle

  • Authorization: the issuer approves and places a hold on the cardholder's funds. Nothing has moved yet.
  • Capture: the merchant claims the authorized amount (immediately for digital goods, typically at shipment for physical goods). Authorizations expire if not captured (the window varies by network and card type).
  • Clearing and settlement: captured transactions are batched, cleared through the networks, and funds settle to the acquirer and then to the merchant, net of fees, usually one or more days later.
  • Refunds, voids, chargebacks: a void cancels an uncaptured authorization; a refund returns captured funds; a chargeback (dispute) is initiated by the cardholder through the issuer and has its own evidence workflow.

3.6 Tokenization and PCI DSS scope

PCI DSS applies to every system component that stores, processes or transmits cardholder data, and to anything connected to those components. The most effective security control is therefore not having the data: card details are entered into PSP-hosted fields (iframes) or a PSP mobile SDK, which return an opaque token (or payment-method ID). Your servers only see the token, so most of your estate falls outside the cardholder-data environment, and your compliance obligations shrink accordingly (the exact self-assessment questionnaire depends on your integration and your acquirer's judgement). Network tokens issued by card schemes go further, replacing the card number even inside the payment chain.

3.7 Video encoding vocabulary

  • Container vs codec: MP4/fMP4 or MPEG-TS are containers; H.264, HEVC, VP9 and AV1 are video codecs; AAC and Opus are audio codecs.
  • Rendition and ladder: one rendition is a (resolution, bitrate, codec) encoding; the ladder is the set of renditions offered for a title.
  • Segment and GOP: streams are split into segments of a few seconds; each segment must start with a keyframe (IDR), so the encoder's GOP structure must align with segment boundaries across renditions to allow seamless switching.
  • Manifest: HLS uses a master playlist (.m3u8) that lists variant playlists; DASH uses an MPD (XML). CMAF lets one set of fMP4 segments serve both.
  • ABR algorithm: the player picks renditions based on throughput estimates and buffer occupancy.
  • DRM: commercial content is encrypted (Common Encryption) and licences are issued by Widevine, FairPlay or PlayReady licence servers.

3.8 Content delivery networks and origin shielding

A CDN caches objects at edge locations near users. For video, the cache hit ratio is everything: a 98% edge hit ratio means origin serves 2% of bytes; 95% means 5% — two and a half times as much. Origin shielding adds a mid-tier cache so that edge misses from many locations collapse into one request to origin. Popular content can be pre-positioned (pushed to edges off-peak), which is the principle behind ISP-embedded appliances.

3.9 Counting at scale

View counts, likes and inventory telemetry share a pattern: very high write rates to few keys. Updating a database row per event fails on hot keys. The standard approach is aggregate-then-write: buffer increments in memory or in a stream processor, flush periodic deltas, and accept that displayed counts lag by seconds. For distinct counts (unique viewers), use probabilistic sketches such as HyperLogLog, which estimate cardinality with about 1–2% error in a few kilobytes. Fraud filtering (bots, replay) determines which views count at all — a policy decision as much as a technical one.

4. Internal Working: Requirements, Estimates, APIs and Data Models

4.1 Case study A — E-commerce platform with flash sales

Functional requirements

  • Catalog: products, variants (SKUs), attributes, images, prices (including promotions), per-region availability.
  • Search and browse: full-text search with facets (brand, size, price range), sorting, typo tolerance, personalised ranking hook.
  • Cart: add/remove/update, guest carts merged on login, price and stock re-validation at checkout.
  • Checkout: address, shipping options, tax, promotions, payment; produces an order.
  • Inventory: per-SKU per-warehouse stock with reservations; never oversell.
  • Orders: lifecycle from placed through paid, fulfilled, shipped, delivered, cancelled, returned; customer order history.
  • Flash sales: scheduled sales of limited stock (e.g. 10,000 units of one SKU) with fair access and bot protection.

Non-functional requirements

  • Product page p99 under 200 ms; search p99 under 300 ms; checkout p99 under 1.5 s (excluding 3-D Secure challenges).
  • Checkout availability 99.95%; catalog/search 99.99% (stale reads acceptable).
  • Zero oversell for limited stock; bounded oversell tolerated for back-orderable stock (business choice).
  • Flash sale: sustain 50× normal traffic on a handful of SKUs for 10 minutes without affecting the rest of the site.
  • Order data durable and auditable for years (tax, consumer law).

Data model

Reading the diagram: stock is per (SKU, warehouse) with three counters, and each reservation is a row with a TTL. Reservations reference a checkout, not a cart: carts are cheap and abandoned constantly; checkouts are short-lived and committed. Orders snapshot the unit price at purchase (unit_price_minor in minor units — cents — as integers, never floating point), because catalog prices change. ORDER_EVENT is the append-only history used for customer timelines, audits and event publishing.

Database choices. Orders, checkouts, reservations and stock belong in a relational database with real transactions — PostgreSQL or MySQL, sharded by user_id for orders (customer history queries stay single-shard) and by sku_id for stock. The catalog's system of record is also relational (merchandisers edit it transactionally), but reads are served from a denormalised document cache and a search index (Elasticsearch/OpenSearch) fed by CDC through Kafka. Carts are a per-user document with high write rates and loose consistency needs: Redis with persistence or DynamoDB, backed up to the user's account. Opinion: resist putting orders in a document store "for flexibility" — you will want joins, constraints and transactions on the day finance asks for a report.

Inventory reservation: three algorithms

  1. Pessimistic row lock — SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, check, update, commit. Correct but serialises all buyers on the row; at 33 K/s with ~2 ms lock hold time you top out near 500/s.
  2. Conditional atomic update — a single statement UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE sku_id = :s AND warehouse_id = :w AND on_hand - reserved - committed >= :q, checking the affected-row count. No read-modify-write race, shorter lock hold, but still one hot row. Good to a few thousand/s per row.
  3. Sharded or pre-allocated counters — split the flash-sale stock into N buckets (e.g. 10,000 units into 100 rows of 100) and have each request pick a random bucket, or hold the sale stock in Redis and decrement with an atomic Lua script, writing reservations to the database asynchronously through a queue. This is the flash-sale design.

Reservations expire. A sweeper (or a delay-queue message scheduled at creation) releases expired reservations with a conditional update WHERE state = 'HELD' AND expires_at < now(), so a late payment success and an expiry cannot both win: whichever transition commits first moves the state, and the other finds zero rows affected and compensates (e.g. a payment that succeeds after expiry is refunded automatically, or re-reserved if stock remains).

Flash sales: admission control first

The key insight: when 2 M people compete for 10 K units, 99.5% of them will fail. The design goal is to make those failures cheap and early rather than letting them reach the database. The layered approach:

  1. Static pre-rendering: the drop page is served from the CDN; nothing dynamic until the sale opens.
  2. Virtual waiting room: at open, each client obtains a queue ticket (signed token with a random or arrival-ordered position) from an edge service. Admission proceeds at a controlled rate (say 2,000/s) that downstream checkout can absorb.
  3. Bot defence: per-account and per-device limits, proof-of-work or CAPTCHA challenges for suspicious clients, purchase limits per customer.
  4. Atomic stock gate: admitted users hit a Redis counter (DECRBY inside a Lua script that refuses to go below zero). Success yields a short-lived reservation token; failure returns "sold out" immediately and closes the waiting room once stock reaches zero.
  5. Durable reservation: the reservation token is written to the database via Kafka by a consumer that processes at a sustainable rate; checkout completion validates the token.
  6. Reconciliation: unclaimed reservations expire and return units to the Redis counter (re-opening the queue if desired); at the end, Redis and database counts are reconciled.

Redis is not your system of record

The Redis counter is an admission gate. If Redis fails over and loses the last few acknowledged decrements (replication is asynchronous), you may admit a few extra buyers. The durable reservation step must therefore also enforce the stock constraint in the database (the conditional update), and the business must decide what happens to the handful of losers there (an apology and a coupon is the usual answer). This two-level design gives Redis speed with database correctness.

Search indexing pipeline

Catalog changes flow from the relational system of record via CDC (Debezium) into Kafka, where an indexer enriches them (category paths, computed availability badges, popularity signals) and writes to the search cluster with versioned upserts (version_type=external using the source row version) so out-of-order events cannot overwrite newer documents. Availability in search results is a badge ("in stock", "low stock"), refreshed every few minutes, not a live count — querying inventory per search hit would couple the search tier to the most contended service in the system.

4.2 Case study B — Payment processing system

We design the payments platform inside a company like the e-commerce business above (or a marketplace): it sits between product services and one or more external PSPs/acquirers. We are not designing a card network or an acquirer.

Functional requirements

  • Create a payment intent for an amount and currency tied to an order; confirm it with a tokenized payment method; support authorize-then-capture and immediate capture.
  • Handle asynchronous steps: 3-D Secure challenges, PSP webhooks, delayed methods (bank transfers, wallets).
  • Refunds (full and partial), voids, and dispute (chargeback) intake.
  • Record every money movement in a double-entry ledger; produce balances for customers (wallet credit), merchants/sellers (payables) and the platform (fees).
  • Reconciliation against PSP settlement reports; payouts to sellers in a marketplace.
  • Fraud hooks: pre-authorization risk scoring, rules, and manual review queue.

Non-functional requirements

  • Exactly-once effect: no double charges under retries, timeouts, crashes or duplicate webhooks.
  • Conservation: the ledger always balances; every external movement is matched during reconciliation.
  • Availability 99.99% for confirm; graceful degradation when a PSP is down (fail over to another PSP for new payments where contracts allow).
  • Latency: confirm p99 under 2 s excluding issuer and challenge time.
  • Auditability: immutable records retained for regulatory periods (often 5–10 years depending on jurisdiction).
  • PCI DSS: no raw card data in our systems; least-privilege access; encryption in transit and at rest.

Payment state machine

Reading the diagram: every arrow is a conditional update in the database (WHERE status = :from), which makes each transition idempotent and race-safe. The PROCESSING state is crucial: it means "we have sent a request to the PSP and do not yet know the outcome". A crash in PROCESSING is resolved not by retrying blindly but by querying the PSP for the result (using the same idempotency key or our reference), which is the essence of making payments self-healing.

Data model and ledger

Reading the diagram: a LEDGER_TXN groups entries that must sum to zero per currency (enforced by a deferred constraint trigger or by the posting service and verified continuously). Accounts are typed: customer_receivable, psp_clearing (money the PSP owes us), seller_payable, platform_fee_revenue, bank_cash. PAYMENT_ATTEMPT exists because one intent may be tried with several payment methods or PSPs, each with its own PSP-side idempotency key. IDEMPOTENCY_KEY stores a hash of the request body so that reuse of a key with a different payload is rejected (422) instead of silently returning the old response.

A captured €45.99 card payment with a 10% platform fee posts, for example: debit psp_clearing 4599; credit seller_payable 4139; credit platform_fee_revenue 460. When the PSP settles to our bank net of its own €0.40 fee: debit bank_cash 4559; debit psp_fee_expense 40; credit psp_clearing 4599. Idempotency mechanics, precisely

The idempotency protocol for a mutating endpoint (adapted from the pattern Stripe documents publicly):

  1. On request, insert (scope, key, request_hash, state='IN_PROGRESS', locked_until=now()+30s). If the insert conflicts:
    • If the stored hash differs → 422 (key reused with different parameters).
    • If state = 'COMPLETED' → return the stored response verbatim.
    • If IN_PROGRESS and the lock is live → 409 "request in progress, retry later".
    • If IN_PROGRESS and the lock expired (previous handler crashed) → take over the lock and resume (look up the payment's state and, if it was in PROCESSING, query the PSP rather than resending).
  2. Execute the operation in steps, persisting "recovery points" (e.g. intent_created, psp_called) so a resumed request knows how far the previous one got.
  3. On completion, store the response code and body with state='COMPLETED'.

Downstream, every PSP call carries our idempotency key (most PSPs accept one), derived deterministically from the attempt (pi_01J…:attempt-1), so even a resent request to the PSP cannot create a second charge.

Webhooks, polling and reconciliation

PSPs report asynchronous outcomes through webhooks, which can arrive late, duplicated or out of order. Handle them by (1) verifying signatures, (2) recording the raw event with a unique constraint on the PSP event ID (dedup), (3) applying it as a conditional state transition, and (4) never trusting a webhook as the only signal — a reconciler polls the PSP for intents stuck in PROCESSING beyond a threshold.

Reconciliation is the daily (or intraday) process that proves our ledger matches reality. Inputs: the PSP's settlement report (each transaction, fee and payout) and our bank statement. Steps: normalise, match on PSP reference (exact match), then on amount/date/currency heuristics for leftovers, classify breaks (missing in ours, missing in theirs, amount mismatch, timing difference), auto-resolve known patterns, and queue the rest for finance operations. Unmatched items age into alerts. Treat reconciliation as a first-class product with its own data pipeline, not a spreadsheet.

Fraud hooks

Before confirming with the PSP, the payments service calls a risk service with order context (account age, device fingerprint, velocity, address mismatch, basket composition). The response is allow, challenge (force 3-D Secure), review (authorize, hold capture, queue for manual review) or block. The call must have a strict timeout with a pre-agreed default (usually allow-with-monitoring for low amounts, review for high amounts), because the fraud service must not become a single point of failure for revenue. Outcomes (chargebacks, confirmed fraud) flow back to label training data.

The transcoding pipeline as a DAG

  1. Probe and validate (FFprobe): container, codecs, duration, resolution, frame rate, corruption checks; reject or normalise.

  5. Stitch and package: concatenate chunk outputs, segment into 2–6 s CMAF segments with aligned keyframes, generate HLS and DASH manifests, apply DRM encryption if required.
  6. Thumbnails, captions, safety checks run as parallel branches.
  7. Publish: write renditions to metadata, flip status to READY through the outbox, warm the CDN for expected-popular content.

5.1 E-commerce — high-level architecture

Reading the diagram: the read path (catalog, search, images) is served mostly from the CDN and caches and is fed asynchronously by CDC, so a slow catalog database cannot break browsing. Key flow: checkout saga

Reading the diagram: the third branch is the one naive implementations miss. A timeout from payments does not mean the payment failed; releasing inventory and telling the customer "payment failed" would invite them to pay again — a double charge. Instead the saga parks in a pending state and resolves by querying the payment status (or waiting for its event). What to try: inject a failure at the payment step and watch the compensations run in reverse (release inventory, cancel order). That asymmetry is why we place the most-likely-to-fail and hardest-to-compensate steps as late as possible but before irreversible external effects such as shipping.

5.2 Payments — high-level architecture

Reading the diagram: card data travels only between the user's device and the PSP's hosted fields; our services handle tokens. That single choice removes the payments API, the databases and most of the estate from the cardholder-data environment. The payments API owns intents and attempts and is the only writer of payment state; it emits events through an outbox, and the ledger service posts balanced transactions from those events — idempotently, keyed on event ID. Reconciliation consumes external settlement files and the ledger, and its breaks surface in an operations console. The PSP router chooses a PSP per payment (cost, currency, card brand, health) and is where failover happens.

Key flow: confirm, authorize, capture, post to ledger

Reading the diagram: three layers of idempotency appear: the client's key at our API (step 2), our key at the PSP (steps 6 and 12), and the event ID at the ledger (step 17). The state change, the outbox record and the idempotency completion commit in one local transaction (step 8), so there is no window where the payment is authorized but no event will ever be emitted. If the process crashes between steps 6 and 8, the intent sits in PROCESSING and the recovery job queries the PSP with the attempt reference — it does not issue a new authorization.

PSP failover and the circuit breaker

Routing new payments to a secondary PSP when the primary is degraded is a major availability lever, but there are subtleties: a token issued by PSP A's vault is usually not usable at PSP B (unless you use network tokens or a third-party vault that can forward card data to multiple PSPs), so failover works only for payment methods available at both. And a payment that already started at PSP A must never be retried at PSP B unless A's attempt is definitively failed — otherwise the customer may be charged by both.

What to try: raise the failure rate past the threshold and watch the breaker open; in the open state, new payments are routed to the secondary PSP immediately instead of waiting for timeouts. Then lower the failure rate and observe half-open probes. Ask yourself which errors should count: a card decline is a successful PSP call with a negative business result and must not trip the breaker; only technical failures (timeouts, 5xx, connection errors) should.

6. Mathematical Foundations

6.1 Contention on a hot row

Intuition: if each transaction holds the row lock for \(t\) seconds, at most \(1/t\) transactions per second can update that row, no matter how many servers you add.

\[ X_{\max} = \frac{1}{t_{\text{lock}}} \]

With a 2 ms lock hold (network round trip to the database plus commit), \(X_{\max} = 500/\text{s}\). Splitting stock into \(B\) buckets raises the ceiling to \(B / t_{\text{lock}}\) — 100 buckets give 50,000/s — at the cost of "false sold-out" when a randomly chosen bucket is empty but others are not (mitigated by retrying another bucket). Moving the gate to Redis reduces \(t\) to tens of microseconds of single-threaded execution.

6.2 Reservation TTL and stock utilisation

If checkouts arrive at rate \(\lambda\) and each holds stock for an average of \(W\) (time to pay or to expire), Little's law gives the stock tied up in reservations:

\[ R = \lambda W \]

At 2,000 admitted checkouts/s with a 10-minute TTL and a 40% abandonment rate, the abandoned ones alone hold \(0.4 \times 2{,}000 \times 600 = 480{,}000\) units-seconds per second, i.e. up to 480,000 units in limbo — far more than 10,000 in stock. Hence flash sales use short TTLs (2–3 minutes) and release reservations eagerly on cancellation.

6.3 Ledger invariants

For every transaction \(T\) and currency \(c\):

\[ \sum_{e \in T,\; e.\text{currency}=c} e.\text{amount} = 0 \]

and the balance of account \(a\) at time \(\tau\) is \(B_a(\tau) = \sum_{e:\,e.\text{account}=a,\; e.t \le \tau} e.\text{amount}\). Because these are sums, they can be checked incrementally (per transaction at write time) and globally (nightly: the sum over all accounts per currency must be zero).

6.4 Adaptive bitrate and bandwidth

Egress volume is watch time times delivered bitrate:

\[ E = T_{\text{watch}} \cdot \bar{b} \]

A codec that reduces bitrate by a fraction \(s\) at equal quality saves \(s \cdot E\) for the content encoded with it. If the extra encoding cost per title is \(C_{\text{enc}}\) and egress costs \(p\) per GB, re-encoding a title pays off when \(s \cdot T_{\text{watch}} \cdot \bar{b} \cdot p \gt C_{\text{enc}}\) — i.e. only for titles with enough watch time. That inequality is the economic logic behind "re-encode popular titles with expensive codecs".

The ABR ladder is a set of points on a rate–quality curve. With quality measured by a perceptual metric \(Q(b)\) that is concave in bitrate, adding rungs where \(Q\) is changing fastest yields the most improvement per bit; per-title encoding fits \(Q(b)\) for each title rather than assuming one curve for all.

6.5 CDN hit ratio and origin load

\[ \text{Origin egress} = (1 - h_{\text{edge}})(1 - h_{\text{shield}}) \cdot E \]

With edge hit ratio 0.97 and shield hit ratio 0.8, origin serves \(0.03 \times 0.2 = 0.6\%\) of bytes. Improving the edge hit ratio from 0.95 to 0.98 cuts origin traffic by 60%.

6.6 Distinct counting with HyperLogLog

HyperLogLog with \(m\) registers estimates the number of distinct items with relative standard error approximately

\[ \sigma \approx \frac{1.04}{\sqrt{m}} \]

Redis uses \(m = 16{,}384\) registers, giving \(\sigma \approx 0.81\%\) in at most 12 KB per key. For unique viewers per video per day, that is a few gigabytes for millions of videos — far smaller than storing viewer sets.

6.7 Probability of a double charge without idempotency

If a fraction \(f\) of PSP calls time out after the PSP has actually processed them (the response was lost), and the client blindly retries, the double-charge rate equals \(f\). Even \(f = 10^{-4}\) on 2 M daily payments is 200 double charges per day — each a support ticket, a refund and a potential chargeback. With idempotency keys at the PSP, the rate drops to zero by construction.

@Service
public class ConfirmPaymentService {
    private final JdbcTemplate jdbc;
    private final TransactionTemplate tx;
    private final PspClient psp;
    private final ObjectMapper json = new ObjectMapper();

    public ConfirmPaymentService(JdbcTemplate jdbc, TransactionTemplate tx, PspClient psp) {
        this.jdbc = jdbc; this.tx = tx; this.psp = psp;
    }

    public ResponseEntity<String> confirm(String scope, String key, String intentId, ConfirmRequest req) {
        String hash = sha256(intentId + "|" + req.paymentMethod());

        // 1. Claim the key, or replay / reject.
        try {
            jdbc.update("""
                INSERT INTO idempotency_key(scope, key, request_hash, state, locked_until)
                VALUES (?, ?, ?, 'IN_PROGRESS', now() + interval '30 seconds')""", scope, key, hash);
        } catch (DuplicateKeyException dup) {
            Map<String, Object> row = jdbc.queryForMap(
                "SELECT request_hash, state, response_code, response_body::text AS body, locked_until > now() AS locked " +
                "FROM idempotency_key WHERE scope = ? AND key = ?", scope, key);
            if (!hash.equals(row.get("request_hash")))
                return ResponseEntity.unprocessableEntity().body("{\"error\":\"idempotency key reused with different request\"}");
            if ("COMPLETED".equals(row.get("state")))
                return ResponseEntity.status((Integer) row.get("response_code")).body((String) row.get("body"));
            if (Boolean.TRUE.equals(row.get("locked")))
                return ResponseEntity.status(409).body("{\"error\":\"request in progress\"}");
            // previous handler died: take over the lock and resume below
            int taken = jdbc.update("""
                UPDATE idempotency_key SET locked_until = now() + interval '30 seconds'
                WHERE scope = ? AND key = ? AND state = 'IN_PROGRESS' AND locked_until <= now()""", scope, key);
            if (taken == 0) return ResponseEntity.status(409).body("{\"error\":\"request in progress\"}");
        }

        // 2. Move intent to PROCESSING exactly once (or find it already there after a crash).
        Map<String, Object> intent = jdbc.queryForMap(
            "SELECT status, amount_minor, currency, attempt_no FROM payment_intent WHERE id = ?", intentId);
        String status = (String) intent.get("status");
        int attempt = (Integer) intent.get("attempt_no");
        PspResult result;
        if ("REQUIRES_PAYMENT_METHOD".equals(status)) {
            int next = attempt + 1;
            int moved = jdbc.update("""
                UPDATE payment_intent SET status = 'PROCESSING', attempt_no = ?, version = version + 1, updated_at = now()
                WHERE id = ? AND status = 'REQUIRES_PAYMENT_METHOD'""", next, intentId);
            if (moved == 0) return ResponseEntity.status(409).body("{\"error\":\"concurrent confirm\"}");
            result = psp.authorize(intentId, (Long) intent.get("amount_minor"), (String) intent.get("currency"),
                                   req.paymentMethod(), intentId + ":" + next);
        } else if ("PROCESSING".equals(status)) {
            result = psp.lookup(intentId + ":" + attempt);     // resume: ask, never resend blindly
        } else {
            return finish(scope, key, intentId, 409, "{\"error\":\"invalid state " + status + "\"}", null, null);
        }

        // 3. Apply the outcome, emit the event and complete the key atomically.
        return switch (result.outcome()) {
            case AUTHORIZED -> finish(scope, key, intentId, 200,
                    "{\"id\":\"" + intentId + "\",\"status\":\"requires_capture\"}", "REQUIRES_CAPTURE", result);
            case DECLINED -> finish(scope, key, intentId, 402,
                    "{\"status\":\"requires_payment_method\",\"declineCode\":\"" + result.declineCode() + "\"}",
                    "REQUIRES_PAYMENT_METHOD", result);
            case REQUIRES_ACTION -> finish(scope, key, intentId, 200,
                    "{\"id\":\"" + intentId + "\",\"status\":\"requires_action\"}", "REQUIRES_ACTION", result);
            case UNKNOWN -> ResponseEntity.accepted()            // key stays IN_PROGRESS; recovery job resolves it
                    .body("{\"id\":\"" + intentId + "\",\"status\":\"processing\"}");
        };
    }

    private ResponseEntity<String> finish(String scope, String key, String intentId, int code, String body,
                                          String newStatus, PspResult r) {
        tx.executeWithoutResult(s -> {
            if (newStatus != null) {
                int n = jdbc.update("""
                    UPDATE payment_intent SET status = ?, psp_reference = ?, version = version + 1, updated_at = now()
                    WHERE id = ? AND status = 'PROCESSING'""", newStatus, r.reference(), intentId);
                if (n == 1) {
                    jdbc.update("INSERT INTO outbox(aggregate_id, type, payload) VALUES (?, ?, ?::jsonb)",
                        intentId, "PaymentIntent." + newStatus, toJson(Map.of("intentId", intentId, "pspRef",
                            String.valueOf(r.reference()))));
                }
            }
            jdbc.update("""
                UPDATE idempotency_key SET state = 'COMPLETED', response_code = ?, response_body = ?::jsonb, locked_until = NULL
                WHERE scope = ? AND key = ?""", code, body, scope, key);
        });
        return ResponseEntity.status(code).body(body);
    }

    private String toJson(Object o) {
        try { return json.writeValueAsString(o); } catch (Exception e) { throw new IllegalStateException(e); }
    }

    private static String sha256(String s) {
        try {
            return HexFormat.of().formatHex(MessageDigest.getInstance("SHA-256").digest(s.getBytes(StandardCharsets.UTF_8)));
        } catch (Exception e) { throw new IllegalStateException(e); }
    }
}

Why the PSP call is outside the database transaction: holding a database transaction (and its connection and row locks) open during a network call to a third party that may take seconds is a classic way to exhaust the Hikari pool under load. Instead, the design uses persisted intermediate state (PROCESSING plus the attempt number) so that any crash between steps is recoverable by lookup. A background job scans for intents in PROCESSING older than a threshold and calls psp.lookup with the same deterministic key.

Spring pitfalls this code avoids

(1) @Transactional around the whole method would hold the transaction during the PSP call. (2) @Retryable on the PSP client without an idempotency key would double-charge on timeouts. (3) Catching DataIntegrityViolationException generically would also swallow check-constraint failures; DuplicateKeyException is the precise subclass for unique-key conflicts.

package dev.book.ledger;

import org.springframework.dao.DuplicateKeyException;
import org.springframework.jdbc.core.JdbcTemplate;
import org.springframework.stereotype.Service;
import org.springframework.transaction.annotation.Transactional;

import java.time.Instant;
import java.util.Comparator;
import java.util.List;
import java.util.Map;
import java.util.stream.Collectors;

record Entry(String accountId, long amountMinor, String currency) {}

@Service
class LedgerPoster {
    private final JdbcTemplate jdbc;
    LedgerPoster(JdbcTemplate jdbc) { this.jdbc = jdbc; }

    /** Idempotent on txnId (use the source event id). Returns false if already posted. */
    @Transactional
    public boolean post(String txnId, String type, String externalRef, Instant effectiveAt, List<Entry> entries) {
        Map<String, Long> sums = entries.stream()
            .collect(Collectors.groupingBy(Entry::currency, Collectors.summingLong(Entry::amountMinor)));
        if (entries.size() < 2 || sums.values().stream().anyMatch(s -> s != 0))
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("unbalanced transaction " + txnId + ": " + sums);

        try {
            jdbc.update("INSERT INTO ledger_txn(txn_id, type, external_ref, effective_at) VALUES (?,?,?,?)",
                txnId, type, externalRef, java.sql.Timestamp.from(effectiveAt));
        } catch (DuplicateKeyException alreadyPosted) {
            return false;
        }
        // Update balances in a stable account order to avoid deadlocks between concurrent postings.
        List<Entry> ordered = entries.stream().sorted(Comparator.comparing(Entry::accountId)).toList();
        int line = 0;
        for (Entry e : ordered) {
            jdbc.update("INSERT INTO ledger_entry(txn_id, line, account_id, amount_minor, currency) VALUES (?,?,?,?,?)",
                txnId, ++line, e.accountId(), e.amountMinor(), e.currency());
            jdbc.update("""
                INSERT INTO account_balance(account_id, balance_minor, version) VALUES (?, ?, 1)
                ON CONFLICT (account_id) DO UPDATE
                SET balance_minor = account_balance.balance_minor + EXCLUDED.balance_minor,
                    version = account_balance.version + 1""", e.accountId(), e.amountMinor());
        }
        return true;
    }
}

7.3 Inventory reservation: SQL for normal stock, Redis Lua for flash sales

package dev.book.inventory;

import org.springframework.data.redis.core.StringRedisTemplate;
import org.springframework.data.redis.core.script.DefaultRedisScript;
import org.springframework.jdbc.core.JdbcTemplate;
import org.springframework.stereotype.Service;
import org.springframework.transaction.annotation.Transactional;

import java.util.List;
import java.util.UUID;

@Service
public class InventoryService {
    private final JdbcTemplate jdbc;
    private final StringRedisTemplate redis;

    // Atomically claim qty units unless that would go below zero; also enforces a per-user limit.
    private static final DefaultRedisScript<Long> CLAIM = new DefaultRedisScript<>("""
        local remaining = tonumber(redis.call('GET', KEYS[1]) or '0')
        local qty = tonumber(ARGV[1])
        local perUser = tonumber(redis.call('HINCRBY', KEYS[2], ARGV[2], qty))
        if perUser > tonumber(ARGV[3]) then
          redis.call('HINCRBY', KEYS[2], ARGV[2], -qty)
          return -2
        end
        if remaining < qty then
          redis.call('HINCRBY', KEYS[2], ARGV[2], -qty)
          return -1
        end
        return redis.call('DECRBY', KEYS[1], qty)
        """, Long.class);

    public InventoryService(JdbcTemplate jdbc, StringRedisTemplate redis) { this.jdbc = jdbc; this.redis = redis; }

    /** Normal path: one conditional UPDATE per line; no read-modify-write race. */
    @Transactional
    public String reserve(String checkoutId, String skuId, String warehouseId, int qty, int ttlSeconds) {
        int updated = jdbc.update("""
            UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + ?, version = version + 1
            WHERE sku_id = ? AND warehouse_id = ? AND on_hand - reserved - committed >= ?""",
            qty, skuId, warehouseId, qty);
        if (updated == 0) throw new OutOfStockException(skuId);
        String id = UUID.randomUUID().toString();
        jdbc.update("""
            INSERT INTO reservation(reservation_id, checkout_id, sku_id, warehouse_id, qty, expires_at, state)
            VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?, ?, now() + make_interval(secs => ?), 'HELD')""",
            id, checkoutId, skuId, warehouseId, qty, ttlSeconds);
        return id;
    }

    /** Release is a guarded state transition, so expiry and payment success cannot both win. */
    @Transactional
    public boolean release(String reservationId) {
        List<Object[]> rows = jdbc.query("""
            UPDATE reservation SET state = 'RELEASED' WHERE reservation_id = ? AND state = 'HELD'
            RETURNING sku_id, warehouse_id, qty""",
            (rs, i) -> new Object[]{rs.getString(1), rs.getString(2), rs.getInt(3)}, reservationId);
        if (rows.isEmpty()) return false;
        Object[] r = rows.get(0);
        jdbc.update("UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved - ? WHERE sku_id = ? AND warehouse_id = ?", r[2], r[0], r[1]);
        return true;
    }

    /** Flash-sale gate: returns remaining units, or -1 sold out, -2 per-user limit exceeded. */
    public long claimFlash(String dropId, String userId, int qty, int perUserLimit) {
        Long r = redis.execute(CLAIM, List.of("drop:" + dropId + ":remaining", "drop:" + dropId + ":users"),
                               String.valueOf(qty), userId, String.valueOf(perUserLimit));
        return r == null ? -1 : r;
    }

    public static class OutOfStockException extends RuntimeException {
        public OutOfStockException(String sku) { super("out of stock: " + sku); }
    }
}

Both keys in the Lua script must hash to the same Redis Cluster slot; in production name them drop:{dropId}:remaining and drop:{dropId}:users so the hash tag {dropId} co-locates them. A successful claim is then published to Kafka and persisted by the reservation writer using the same conditional UPDATE, which remains the final arbiter of stock.

7.4 View-count ingestion and aggregation in Go

A compact ingest service: it accepts batched player events, applies the counting policy (a view counts once per viewer per video per 30 minutes after 30 s of watch time — an illustrative policy), aggregates increments in memory, and flushes deltas to Redis every few seconds with one pipelined round trip. Unique viewers are tracked with Redis HyperLogLog. In the full architecture this logic lives in a stream processor reading Kafka; the in-process version shows the core mechanics. Module: github.com/redis/go-redis/v9.

package main

import (
	"context"
	"encoding/json"
	"log"
	"net/http"
	"sync"
	"time"

	"github.com/redis/go-redis/v9"
)

type Event struct {
	VideoID     string `json:"videoId"`
	ViewerID    string `json:"viewerId"` // user id or device id
	WatchedSecs int    `json:"watchedSecs"`
}

type Aggregator struct {
	rdb     *redis.Client
	mu      sync.Mutex
	pending map[string]int64    // videoId -> counted views since last flush
	uniques map[string][]string // videoId -> viewer ids since last flush (for HLL)
}

const (
	minWatch   = 30
	dedupTTL   = 30 * time.Minute
	flushEvery = 5 * time.Second
)

// Count applies the policy. The dedup marker uses SET NX so only the first qualifying
// event per viewer/video/window is counted, across all ingest replicas.
func (a *Aggregator) Count(ctx context.Context, evs []Event) error {
	pipe := a.rdb.Pipeline()
	type pendingCmd struct {
		ev  Event
		cmd *redis.BoolCmd
	}
	var cmds []pendingCmd
	for _, e := range evs {
		if e.WatchedSecs < minWatch || e.VideoID == "" || e.ViewerID == "" {
			continue
		}
		key := "vdedup:" + e.VideoID + ":" + e.ViewerID
		cmds = append(cmds, pendingCmd{e, pipe.SetNX(ctx, key, 1, dedupTTL)})
	}
	if len(cmds) == 0 {
		return nil
	}
	if _, err := pipe.Exec(ctx); err != nil && err != redis.Nil {
		return err
	}
	a.mu.Lock()
	defer a.mu.Unlock()
	for _, c := range cmds {
		if c.cmd.Val() { // first time in this window
			a.pending[c.ev.VideoID]++
			a.uniques[c.ev.VideoID] = append(a.uniques[c.ev.VideoID], c.ev.ViewerID)
		}
	}
	return nil
}

// Flush swaps the maps and writes all deltas in a single pipeline.
func (a *Aggregator) Flush(ctx context.Context) {
	a.mu.Lock()
	pending, uniques := a.pending, a.uniques
	a.pending, a.uniques = make(map[string]int64), make(map[string][]string)
	a.mu.Unlock()
	if len(pending) == 0 {
		return
	}
	day := time.Now().UTC().Format("20060102")
	pipe := a.rdb.Pipeline()
	for vid, n := range pending {
		pipe.IncrBy(ctx, "views:"+vid, n)
		pipe.IncrBy(ctx, "views:"+vid+":"+day, n)
		ids := make([]interface{}, len(uniques[vid]))
		for i, v := range uniques[vid] {
			ids[i] = v
		}
		pipe.PFAdd(ctx, "uniq:"+vid+":"+day, ids...)
	}
	if _, err := pipe.Exec(ctx); err != nil {
		log.Printf("flush failed, re-queueing %d videos: %v", len(pending), err)
		a.mu.Lock()
		for vid, n := range pending { // put deltas back; counts are approximate on crash, exact in the warehouse
			a.pending[vid] += n
			a.uniques[vid] = append(a.uniques[vid], uniques[vid]...)
		}
		a.mu.Unlock()
	}
}

func main() {
	ctx := context.Background()
	agg := &Aggregator{
		rdb:     redis.NewClient(&redis.Options{Addr: "localhost:6379"}),
		pending: make(map[string]int64),
		uniques: make(map[string][]string),
	}
	go func() {
		t := time.NewTicker(flushEvery)
		for range t.C {
			agg.Flush(ctx)
		}
	}()
	http.HandleFunc("/v1/view-events", func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
		var evs []Event
		if err := json.NewDecoder(http.MaxBytesReader(w, r.Body, 1<<20)).Decode(&evs); err != nil {
			http.Error(w, "bad request", http.StatusBadRequest)
			return
		}
		if err := agg.Count(r.Context(), evs); err != nil {
			http.Error(w, "unavailable", http.StatusServiceUnavailable)
			return
		}
		w.WriteHeader(http.StatusAccepted)
	})
	log.Fatal(http.ListenAndServe(":8081", nil))
}

A viral video at 100 K qualifying events/s produces one INCRBY per ingest replica every 5 seconds instead of 100 K writes per second. The dedup SETNX keys are the remaining per-event cost; at larger scale replace them with keyed state in a stream processor (Flink keyed by (videoId, viewerId) with a TTL), which is what Chapter 2.12's analytics platform does.

7.5 Building an ABR ladder with FFmpeg from Python

This script probes the source, drops rungs above the source resolution, and builds one FFmpeg invocation that produces aligned-keyframe fMP4 HLS renditions plus a master playlist. Keyframes are forced every segment duration, independent of frame rate, so all renditions switch cleanly. Requires FFmpeg 6+ with libx264 on PATH.

"""Encode an HLS (fMP4/CMAF) ABR ladder. Usage: python ladder.py input.mp4 out_dir"""
from __future__ import annotations

import json
import subprocess
import sys
from dataclasses import dataclass
from pathlib import Path

SEGMENT_SECONDS = 4


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Rung:
    height: int
    video_kbps: int
    audio_kbps: int


LADDER = [  # illustrative fixed ladder; per-title encoding would compute this per source
    Rung(360, 800, 96),
    Rung(540, 1400, 96),
    Rung(720, 2800, 128),
    Rung(1080, 5000, 128),
]


def probe_height(src: Path) -> int:
    out = subprocess.run(
        ["ffprobe", "-v", "error", "-select_streams", "v:0", "-show_entries", "stream=height",
         "-of", "json", str(src)],
        check=True, capture_output=True, text=True,
    ).stdout
    return int(json.loads(out)["streams"][0]["height"])


def build_command(src: Path, out: Path, rungs: list[Rung]) -> list[str]:
    n = len(rungs)
    split = f"[0:v]split={n}" + "".join(f"[v{i}]" for i in range(n))
    scales = ";".join(f"[v{i}]scale=-2:{r.height}[v{i}o]" for i, r in enumerate(rungs))
    cmd = ["ffmpeg", "-y", "-i", str(src), "-filter_complex", f"{split};{scales}"]
    for i, r in enumerate(rungs):
        cmd += [
            "-map", f"[v{i}o]", f"-c:v:{i}", "libx264", f"-b:v:{i}", f"{r.video_kbps}k",
            f"-maxrate:v:{i}", f"{int(r.video_kbps * 1.07)}k", f"-bufsize:v:{i}", f"{int(r.video_kbps * 1.5)}k",
            "-map", "a:0?", f"-c:a:{i}", "aac", f"-b:a:{i}", f"{r.audio_kbps}k", "-ac", "2",
        ]
    cmd += [
        "-preset", "medium", "-profile:v", "high",
        "-force_key_frames", f"expr:gte(t,n_forced*{SEGMENT_SECONDS})", "-sc_threshold", "0",
        "-f", "hls", "-hls_time", str(SEGMENT_SECONDS), "-hls_playlist_type", "vod",
        "-hls_segment_type", "fmp4", "-hls_flags", "independent_segments",
        "-master_pl_name", "master.m3u8",
        "-var_stream_map", " ".join(f"v:{i},a:{i}" for i in range(n)),
        str(out / "%v" / "index.m3u8"),
    ]
    return cmd


def main() -> None:
    src, out = Path(sys.argv[1]), Path(sys.argv[2])
    out.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    src_h = probe_height(src)
    rungs = [r for r in LADDER if r.height <= src_h] or LADDER[:1]   # never upscale
    cmd = build_command(src, out, rungs)
    print(" ".join(cmd))
    subprocess.run(cmd, check=True)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

In the real pipeline this runs per chunk on many workers, and a separate packaging step concatenates chunk outputs and writes manifests; the single-process version is ideal for local experiments and for understanding the knobs. Note the security point from §5.3: FFmpeg parses untrusted input, so production workers run it in sandboxed containers without network access.

9. Case Studies: What Real Companies Did

9.1 Amazon Dynamo and the always-writable cart Verified

Amazon's SOSP 2007 paper describes Dynamo, a key-value store designed so that services such as the shopping cart could always accept writes, using consistent hashing, sloppy quorums, hinted handoff and vector clocks, with divergent versions reconciled by the application (for the cart, by merging). It is the canonical example of choosing availability for a commerce workload. Source: Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store (SOSP 2007).

9.2 Shopify: surviving flash sales Verified

Shopify's 2017 two-part series explains how it built queueing and throttling for write-heavy flash-sale traffic into scriptable (Nginx + Lua / OpenResty) load balancers, because provisioning permanent capacity for rare spikes was not economical — the same "admission control at the edge" principle as our waiting room. Shopify has also described its "pods" architecture isolating groups of shops. Sources: Surviving Flashes of High-Write Traffic Using Scriptable Load Balancers, Part I and Part II.

9.3 Stripe: idempotency keys Verified

12. Common Mistakes and Production Failures

  • Read-then-write stock checks (if (stock > 0) stock-- in Java): oversells under concurrency even inside @Transactional at READ COMMITTED.
  • Floating-point money: double amounts produce rounding drift; use integer minor units or BigDecimal with explicit scale and rounding mode.
  • Treating a timeout as a decline: the customer retries and is charged twice.
  • Calling the PSP inside a database transaction: connection-pool exhaustion when the PSP slows down.
  • Mutable balance columns without entries: impossible to audit or reconcile; one bug silently corrupts money.
  • Trusting webhooks blindly: unsigned webhooks let anyone mark orders paid; unordered webhooks regress states.
  • Logging request bodies on payment endpoints: card data or tokens in logs expands PCI scope and leaks secrets.
  • Circuit breakers counting declines as failures: a fraud wave trips the breaker and fails over healthy traffic.
  • Proxying video bytes through app servers: NIC-bound servers and enormous bills; use pre-signed URLs and CDNs.
  • Per-user tokens in segment URLs: every viewer gets a distinct cache key, and the CDN hit ratio collapses.
  • Unaligned keyframes: visible glitches on every bitrate switch.
  • Updating a counter row per view: hot-row contention on viral videos.

14. Exercises

Exercise 1 · Foundational

A product page shows "only 3 left". Two users click "Buy" at the same instant for 2 units each. Walk through what happens with (a) read-then-write in Java under READ COMMITTED, (b) the conditional UPDATE of §7.3.

Solution

(a) Both transactions read available = 3, both decide 2 ≤ 3, both write available = 1 (or reserved += 2 twice): one sale is lost or 4 units are sold from 3 — an oversell. READ COMMITTED does not prevent this lost-update anomaly. (b) The first UPDATE locks the row and succeeds (reserved becomes 2); the second waits on the row lock, then re-evaluates the WHERE clause against the committed row (PostgreSQL re-checks the predicate on the updated row version), finds available = 1 < 2, updates 0 rows, and the application reports out of stock.

Exercise 2 · Intermediate

Write the ledger entries for: a €100 order with a 15% platform fee; a partial refund of €20 (platform refunds its fee proportionally); and the PSP settling the net amount minus a €1.20 processing fee. Show that each transaction balances and compute the final balances of every account.

Solution

Capture: psp_clearing +10000; seller_payable −8500; fee_revenue −1500. Refund: seller_payable +1700; fee_revenue +300; psp_clearing −2000. Settlement of net 8000 minus 120 fee: bank_cash +7880; psp_fee_expense +120; psp_clearing −8000. Each sums to zero. Final: psp_clearing 0; seller_payable −6800 (we owe the seller €68); fee_revenue −1200 (€12 revenue, credit-normal); bank_cash +7880; psp_fee_expense +120. Check: 7880 + 120 − 6800 − 1200 = 0. Exercise 3 · Intermediate

Your idempotency table keeps keys for 24 hours. A client retries a confirm 30 hours later with the same key. What happens, and how would you prevent a double charge in that case?

Solution

The key has expired, so the request is treated as new. Protection comes from the second layer: the payment intent's state machine. The intent is no longer in REQUIRES_PAYMENT_METHOD (it is REQUIRES_CAPTURE or SUCCEEDED), so the conditional transition fails and the API returns 409/400 "invalid state" instead of charging. This is why idempotency should never rely on a single mechanism: natural keys (one intent per order, enforced by a unique constraint) and state guards are the durable backstop.

Exercise 4 · Advanced

Design reconciliation for a PSP settlement file of 2 million rows per day against your ledger. Specify matching keys, break categories, and how you would make the job idempotent and re-runnable.

Solution

Load the file into a staging table keyed by (file_id, row_no) with a checksum (re-loading the same file is a no-op). Normalise (currency, sign, timestamps). Pass 1: exact match on PSP reference to ledger external_ref and amount. Pass 2: for unmatched, match on (amount, currency, date ± 1 day, last4/BIN) with uniqueness checks. Categories: missing internally (PSP charged, we never recorded — critical), missing externally (we think captured, PSP has nothing — check timing), amount mismatch (fees, FX), timing (will match tomorrow), duplicates. Persist results as a reconciliation run with versioned rules; re-running a day replaces that day's results transactionally. Alert on critical categories and on unmatched value above thresholds; age timing breaks and escalate after N days.

Exercise 5 · Advanced

A title will be watched for 50 million hours in its first month at an average of 4 Mbit/s with H.264. AV1 saves 30% bitrate at equal quality. Encoding the AV1 ladder costs an extra 20,000 core-hours at $0.02 per core-hour; egress costs $0.002 per GB. Is it worth it for this title? At what watch time does it break even?

Solution

Egress at H.264: 50 × 10⁶ h × 3600 s × 4 Mbit/s / 8 = 9 × 10¹⁰ MB = 90 PB = 9 × 10⁷ GB → $180,000. AV1 saves 30% (assuming most devices can play it) = $54,000. Encoding costs 20,000 × $0.02 = $400. Clearly worth it. Break-even: savings proportional to watch time; $400 / $54,000 × 50 M h ≈ 370,000 hours. Titles below that (most of the long tail) are not worth AV1 at these prices. Adjust for the fraction of AV1-capable devices. Illustrative

Exercise 6 · Expert

Extend the payment design to a marketplace where one checkout pays three sellers, one of which later cancels its part. Describe the payment, ledger and payout implications.

Solution

Use one payment intent for the customer (one card charge) and allocate it in the ledger to three seller payables plus fees in one balanced transaction (split payment). Seller cancellation triggers a partial refund of that seller's portion: reverse that seller's payable and the associated fee, credit psp_clearing. Payouts are computed from payable balances after a hold period (to cover refunds and chargebacks), so the cancelled portion never leaves. If the cancellation happens after payout, the seller's payable goes negative and is recovered from future payouts (or invoiced). Regulatory note: holding funds for third parties may require licensing or using a PSP's marketplace product that handles fund flows Version-sensitive · checked Nov 2026.

15. Interview Questions and Answers

Architecture How do you prevent overselling during a flash sale with 100× normal traffic on one SKU?

Admission control first (CDN-served page, virtual waiting room admitting at a sustainable rate, bot defences, per-customer limits); then an atomic in-memory gate (Redis Lua decrement that refuses below zero) to fail most attempts in milliseconds; then durable reservations written asynchronously with the database's conditional update as final arbiter; short TTLs and eager releases; reconciliation at the end. Explain why Redis alone is insufficient (async replication) and what happens to the rare loser.

Conceptual What is an idempotency key, where is it stored, and what does it protect against?

A client-generated unique value sent with a mutating request. The server records it with a request fingerprint and the response, atomically with (or recoverably around) the side effect, and replays the response for duplicates. It protects against duplicates caused by client retries after timeouts or dropped connections. It does not protect against a client generating a new key for the same intent — natural keys and state guards cover that.

Scenario A PSP call times out during authorization. What do you do?

Do not report failure or retry blindly. Keep the intent in PROCESSING, return "processing" to the caller, and resolve by querying the PSP using the same idempotency key/reference (immediately with backoff and via a recovery job), or wait for the webhook. If the PSP confirms authorization, continue; if it has no record, it is then safe to retry with the same key.

Conceptual Why use double-entry bookkeeping instead of a balance column?

Because every change becomes an immutable, balanced record: you can audit how a balance came to be, replay history, prove conservation (sum of all accounts is zero), and reconcile against external statements. A mutable balance column loses history and makes bugs silently destructive.

Architecture How does tokenization reduce PCI DSS scope?

Card data is collected by PSP-hosted fields or SDKs directly into the PSP; your systems receive only tokens, which are not cardholder data in the PCI sense. Systems that never store, process or transmit card data (and are not connected to systems that do) are out of the cardholder-data environment, so far fewer controls apply. Scope still includes the pages that embed the hosted fields (script integrity matters) — confirm the exact SAQ with your acquirer.

Architecture Design the upload path for 50 GB video files from flaky mobile connections.

Multipart/resumable upload direct to object storage with pre-signed part URLs (e.g. 64 MB parts), client-side checksums per part, a server-side session that tracks completed parts so the client can resume after app restarts, completion call that validates all parts and the overall hash, and processing triggered by the storage event. Application servers never touch bytes.

Conceptual What is adaptive bitrate streaming and why must keyframes be aligned?

The video is encoded at multiple bitrates/resolutions and segmented; the player switches between renditions per segment based on throughput and buffer. Each segment must begin with a keyframe at the same timestamps across renditions so that a segment from any rendition can follow a segment from any other without decoding errors or visual glitches.

Scenario Your CDN bill doubled after a release though traffic was flat. What happened?

Most likely the cache hit ratio dropped: a change introduced per-user or per-session query parameters into segment URLs (cache-key fragmentation), changed cache headers, or broke origin shielding. Check hit ratio by path, compare URL formats before/after, and fix by moving auth into signed cookies or token-in-path schemes the CDN normalises.

Coding Write a SQL statement that reserves stock safely without explicit locks.

UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE sku_id = :s AND warehouse_id = :w AND on_hand - reserved - committed >= :q, then check that exactly one row was updated. The row lock taken by UPDATE and the predicate re-check make it race-free; insert the reservation row in the same transaction.

Behavioral Tell me about a time you found a data-integrity bug in production. How did you limit the damage?

Strong answers cover detection (an invariant check or reconciliation break), immediate containment (feature flag, stop payouts), quantification (query the blast radius), customer remediation, root cause (e.g. a missing idempotency key on a retry path), and systemic prevention (invariant monitors, tests that inject duplicates and timeouts).
