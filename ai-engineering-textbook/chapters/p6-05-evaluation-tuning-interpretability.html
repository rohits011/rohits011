Part 6 · Chapter 5

Evaluation, Bias–Variance, Cross-Validation, Tuning & Interpretability

A model is only as trustworthy as the measurement behind it. This chapter is the test suite of machine learning: which metric answers which business question, why ROC-AUC can look great while the product fails, how to derive and use the bias–variance decomposition, which cross-validation scheme matches your data, how to search hyperparameters efficiently, and how to explain a model's predictions to a regulator, a product manager or yourself at 2 a.m.

  • Prerequisites: Chapters 6.1–6.3 (expectation and variance, confidence intervals, logistic regression, tree ensembles) and 6.2 (splits and leakage).
  • You will be able to: choose and compute regression, classification, calibration and ranking metrics; pick thresholds from costs; derive the bias–variance decomposition; design k-fold, stratified, grouped and time-series cross-validation; run grid, random and Bayesian (Optuna) searches with early stopping; produce permutation importance, partial dependence, SHAP and LIME explanations and know their failure modes; and run basic fairness checks.

1. Introduction: Measuring What Matters

You would never ship a service whose only test was "it compiles". Yet ML teams routinely ship models validated by one number on one split. Evaluation answers three different questions, and confusing them causes most failures:

  1. Is this model good at its task? Offline metrics on held-out data: RMSE, PR-AUC, NDCG.
  2. Is it better than the alternative, reliably? Confidence intervals, cross-validation variance, paired comparisons (Chapter 6.1's statistics).
  3. Does it improve the product? Online experiments on business metrics (conversion, losses prevented, time saved), which offline metrics only approximate.

Around these sit two more concerns. Generalisation: the model fits the training data, but will it fit the future? That is the domain of overfitting, the bias–variance trade-off, regularisation and cross-validation. Understanding: why does it predict what it predicts? That is interpretability, needed for debugging, trust, regulation and fairness.

Bridging from Java & Spring Boot

What you already know
JUnit and Testcontainers, contract tests, load tests with Gatling/k6 reporting p50/p99, canary deployments gated by error-rate SLOs, and A/B flags. You know a test is only meaningful if it exercises realistic inputs and that a green build is not the same as a happy customer.
Why that is not enough
ML correctness is statistical, not boolean. A model is never "correct"; it has an error distribution. Unit tests cannot tell you whether 0.87 AUC is good enough, whether it will hold next month, or whether errors concentrate on one customer group. And the test data itself can leak or be unrepresentative.
The modern approach
Define metrics from business costs before training; use cross-validation matched to the data's structure; report uncertainty; tune with efficient search and early stopping; slice metrics by segment; check calibration; explain with model-agnostic tools (permutation importance, SHAP); and gate releases on both offline evaluation and online experiments, like a canary.
Where Python and Go fit

In production
An evaluation report (metrics with intervals, per-segment slices, calibration plot, top features, fairness table) is generated for every candidate model in CI, stored with the model in the registry, and compared automatically with the champion. Explanations may be returned with predictions (reason codes for credit decisions).
Use it when / avoid it when
Always. The only thing to avoid is evaluation theatre: tuning on the test set, reporting the best of many runs, or choosing a metric after seeing results.

3. Fundamentals

3.1 Regression metrics

Suppose a delivery-time model predicts [30, 25, 40, 50] minutes and actual times were [28, 30, 38, 70].

  • MAE (mean absolute error): average size of the miss. |2| + |−5| + |2| + |−20| = 29, /4 = 7.25 minutes. Easy to explain; every minute of error counts equally; optimal constant prediction is the median.
  • MSE / RMSE: average squared miss, and its square root to return to minutes. Squares: 4, 25, 4, 400 → MSE 108.25, RMSE ≈ 10.40. The single 20-minute miss dominates. Use when large errors are disproportionately bad; optimal constant is the mean.
  • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error): 2/28, 5/30, 2/38, 20/70 = 7.1%, 16.7%, 5.3%, 28.6% → 14.4%. Scale-free, but undefined at zero targets and asymmetric (penalises over-prediction of small values heavily). Prefer weighted variants (WAPE = Σ|e| / Σ|y| = 29/166 ≈ 17.5%) for demand data.
  • R²: fraction of variance explained compared with predicting the mean. R² = 1 − SSE/SST. Here ȳ = 41.5, SST = 182.25 + 132.25 + 12.25 + 812.25 = 1139; SSE = 433; R² = 1 − 433/1139 ≈ 0.62. R² can be negative on test data (worse than the mean) and is not comparable across datasets.
  • Quantile (pinball) loss: for predicting a percentile (an ETA you will be on time for 90% of the time), penalise under- and over-prediction asymmetrically.
\[ \text{MAE} = \frac1m\sum|y_i-\hat y_i|,\quad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac1m\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2},\quad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}. \]

3.2 The confusion matrix

For a binary classifier with a threshold, every prediction lands in one of four cells. Fraud example on 10,000 transactions with 100 frauds; the model flags 150, of which 80 are fraud:

Predicted fraudPredicted legitTotal
Actual fraudTP = 80FN = 20100
Actual legitFP = 70TN = 9,8309,900
Total1509,85010,000

From it:

  • Accuracy = (TP + TN)/total = 9,910/10,000 = 99.1%. Looks superb; "always legit" scores 99.0%. Useless under imbalance.
  • Precision = TP/(TP + FP) = 80/150 = 53.3%. Of the flagged, how many are real? The cost of false alarms (customer friction, analyst time).
  • Recall (sensitivity, TPR) = TP/(TP + FN) = 80/100 = 80%. Of the real frauds, how many did we catch? The cost of misses.
  • Specificity = TN/(TN + FP) = 9,830/9,900 = 99.3%; false positive rate = 1 − specificity = 0.71%.
  • F1 = harmonic mean of precision and recall = 2PR/(P + R) = 2 × 0.533 × 0.8 / 1.333 = 0.64. The harmonic mean is dragged toward the smaller value, so F1 is high only when both are. Fβ weights recall β times as much as precision.
\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad F_\beta = (1+\beta^2)\frac{P\cdot R}{\beta^2P + R}. \]

For multiclass problems, compute per-class precision/recall and average: macro (unweighted mean over classes, treats rare classes equally), weighted (by class support), or micro (pool all decisions; equals accuracy for single-label multiclass).

3.3 Thresholds, ROC curves and ROC-AUC

A probabilistic classifier produces a score; the threshold turns it into a decision, and every threshold gives a different confusion matrix. The ROC curve plots recall (TPR) against FPR as the threshold sweeps from 1 to 0. A random classifier traces the diagonal; a perfect one hugs the top-left corner.

ROC-AUC is the area under that curve, with a beautiful interpretation: the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored higher than a randomly chosen negative. Example: positives scored [0.9, 0.6], negatives [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]. Of the 2 × 3 = 6 pairs, positive wins: (0.9 > 0.7, 0.2, 0.1) = 3; (0.6 > 0.2, 0.1) = 2, loses to 0.7. AUC = 5/6 ≈ 0.833. It measures ranking quality, independent of threshold and of class balance.

3.4 Precision-recall curves and PR-AUC

That independence from class balance is a problem when positives are rare. With 1% fraud, an FPR of 1% means as many false alarms as there are frauds: precision near 50% even with perfect recall. ROC-AUC barely notices, because FPR divides by the huge number of negatives. The precision-recall curve plots precision against recall across thresholds; its area, PR-AUC (often computed as average precision), is sensitive to exactly the false-alarm burden you care about. The baseline is the positive rate (0.01 here), not 0.5.

Rule of thumb: ROC-AUC for balanced problems or when both error types matter similarly; PR-AUC for rare-positive detection (fraud, defects, search relevance). A model can move from ROC-AUC 0.95 to 0.96 while PR-AUC moves from 0.30 to 0.45, a large operational difference.

3.5 Choosing a threshold from costs

The right threshold is a business calculation. Suppose a missed fraud costs $200 and a false alarm (a blocked legitimate customer) costs $10. If the model is calibrated, flag a transaction when the expected cost of letting it through exceeds the expected cost of blocking it: p × 200 > (1 − p) × 10, i.e. p > 10/210 ≈ 0.048. Not 0.5. In general, with cost cFN of a miss and cFP of a false alarm:

\[ \text{flag if } p > \frac{c_{FP}}{c_{FP} + c_{FN}}. \]

When the constraint is operational (analysts can review 500 cases per day), choose the threshold that yields 500 flags per day on recent traffic, and report precision and recall at that operating point. Always report metrics at the operating threshold, not just threshold-free AUCs.

3.6 Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among examples to which it assigns probability 0.3, about 30% are positive. Ranking metrics (AUC) ignore calibration entirely: multiply every logit by 10 and AUC is unchanged while the probabilities become absurdly extreme. Calibration matters whenever the probability is used as a number: expected-cost thresholds (above), pricing, bidding, combining models, or showing "70% chance" to users.

Diagnose with a reliability diagram: bin predictions (0–0.1, 0.1–0.2, …), plot mean predicted probability against observed positive rate per bin; a calibrated model lies on the diagonal. Summaries: the Brier score, mean squared error between probability and outcome (lower is better, and it rewards both calibration and sharpness), log-loss, and expected calibration error (ECE, the bin-weighted average gap). Example: predictions [0.9, 0.8, 0.3] for outcomes [1, 0, 0]: Brier = (0.01 + 0.64 + 0.09)/3 ≈ 0.247.

Fix with post-hoc calibration on held-out data: Platt scaling fits a logistic regression on the model's score (two parameters, good for small data, assumes a sigmoid-shaped distortion); isotonic regression fits a non-decreasing step function (flexible, needs more data). CalibratedClassifierCV does either with cross-validation. Class weighting and resampling (Chapter 6.2) distort calibration; random forests and SVMs are typically poorly calibrated out of the box; logistic regression is usually well calibrated.

3.7 Ranking metrics

Search, recommendations and retrieval are judged by the order of results, and users only look at the top. Given a ranked list where relevant items are at positions 1, 3 and 6 out of 10, with 4 relevant items in total:

  • Precision@k: fraction of the top k that are relevant. P@5 = 2/5 = 0.4.
  • Recall@k: fraction of all relevant items in the top k. R@5 = 2/4 = 0.5. The standard metric for the retrieval stage of a RAG system or a candidate generator.
  • MRR (mean reciprocal rank): 1/rank of the first relevant result, averaged over queries. Here 1/1 = 1.0. Good when one right answer suffices.
  • MAP (mean average precision): average of precision at each relevant position: (1/1 + 2/3 + 3/6)/4 relevant = (1 + 0.667 + 0.5)/4 ≈ 0.542 (the missing fourth contributes 0).
  • NDCG@k: handles graded relevance (0 = irrelevant, 3 = perfect) and discounts lower positions logarithmically. DCG@k = Σi≤k (2reli − 1)/log₂(i + 1), normalised by the DCG of the ideal ordering, so NDCG ∈ [0, 1].
\[ \text{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{\text{rel}_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)},\qquad \text{NDCG@}k = \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k}. \]

Example with graded relevances [3, 0, 2] at positions 1–3: DCG = 7/1 + 0/1.585 + 3/2 = 8.5. Ideal order [3, 2, 0]: 7 + 3/1.585 + 0 = 8.893. NDCG@3 = 0.956.

3.8 Overfitting and underfitting

Underfitting: the model is too simple to capture the pattern; training and validation error are both high and close together. A straight line through a curved relationship. Overfitting: the model has learned noise specific to the training set; training error is low but validation error is much higher. A degree-15 polynomial threading through 20 noisy points. The diagnosis comes from comparing training and validation curves:

SymptomDiagnosisRemedies
High train error, high val error, small gapUnderfitting (high bias)More features, more flexible model, less regularisation, train longer
Low train error, high val error, large gapOverfitting (high variance)More data, regularisation, simpler model, early stopping, bagging, feature selection, augmentation
Val error falls with more data (learning curve)Variance-limitedCollect more data
Val error flat with more data, close to trainBias-limitedBetter features/model; more data will not help

3.9 The bias–variance trade-off

Intuition. Imagine retraining the same model type on many different training sets drawn from the same source and looking at its prediction at one fixed input. Two things can go wrong. The average prediction can be off target: that is bias, error from the model family's wrong assumptions (a line cannot fit a curve however much data you have). The predictions can scatter from one training set to another: that is variance, error from sensitivity to the particular sample (a deep tree changes completely when a few rows change). On top of both sits irreducible noise: the target itself is random given the features, and no model can predict it.

For squared error, these three add up exactly (derived in §6):

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat f(\mathbf{x}))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(\mathbf{x})] - f(\mathbf{x})\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(\mathbf{x})])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}. \]

Archery analogy: bias is how far the centre of your arrow cluster is from the bullseye; variance is how spread the cluster is. Increasing model flexibility typically lowers bias and raises variance, so test error traces a U-shape against complexity, with the sweet spot in the middle. Regularisation, ensembling and more data move you around this picture: regularisation trades a little bias for a lot less variance; bagging reduces variance; boosting reduces bias; more data reduces variance.

Double descent

Large neural networks complicate the classic U-curve: test error can fall again as parameters grow far beyond the number of training points ("double descent", Belkin et al., 2019; Nakkiran et al., 2019). The decomposition still holds for squared error; what changes is how variance behaves for very over-parameterised models trained with gradient descent and implicit regularisation. For the tabular models in Part 6, the classic picture is the right working model.

3.10 Regularisation revisited: one idea, many forms

Regularisation is anything that restricts effective model complexity to reduce variance: penalties on weights (L1/L2, Chapter 6.3), tree constraints (depth, min leaf size, γ), shrinkage in boosting, early stopping, dropout and weight decay in neural networks (Chapter 7.1), data augmentation, and ensembling. All of them are "complexity budgets", and all have a strength hyperparameter that must be tuned on validation data, never on the test set.

3.11 Cross-validation

A single validation split gives a noisy estimate, especially on small data. k-fold cross-validation splits the training data into k folds, trains k models each holding out one fold, and averages the k validation scores. Every example is used for validation exactly once, and the spread of fold scores estimates uncertainty. k = 5 or 10 is typical. Variants match the data's structure:

  • Stratified k-fold: each fold preserves class proportions. Default for classification.
  • Group k-fold: all rows of a group (user, patient, device, session) land in the same fold. Mandatory when the model will face new groups in production.
  • Time-series split: folds are expanding (or sliding) windows; train on the past, validate on the next block, optionally with a gap. Never shuffle time-dependent data.
  • Repeated k-fold: repeat with different shuffles to reduce estimate variance.
  • Nested cross-validation: an outer loop estimates performance, an inner loop tunes hyperparameters within each outer training fold. Without nesting, the CV score of the best configuration found by tuning on the same folds is optimistically biased.
  • Leave-one-out: k = m; nearly unbiased, high variance and expensive; rarely worth it.

Cross-validation evaluates a procedure (preprocessing + model + hyperparameters). After choosing, refit the procedure on all training data and evaluate once on the untouched test set.

3.12 Hyperparameter tuning

Hyperparameters are settings not learned by training (learning rate, depth, regularisation strength). Tuning is an outer optimisation over a noisy, expensive black box: each evaluation is a full cross-validated training run.

  • Grid search: try every combination of listed values. Simple and parallel, but cost multiplies with each parameter, and it wastes evaluations on unimportant dimensions.
  • Random search: sample configurations from distributions (log-uniform for learning rates and regularisation). Bergstra and Bengio showed that, because usually only a few hyperparameters matter, random search explores the important ones far more efficiently: with 9 trials, a 3 × 3 grid tests only 3 distinct values of the important parameter; random search tests 9.
  • Bayesian optimisation: build a cheap surrogate model of score as a function of hyperparameters from completed trials, and pick the next trial by maximising an acquisition function that balances exploring uncertain regions and exploiting promising ones. Optuna's default sampler, TPE (tree-structured Parzen estimator), models the densities of good and bad configurations and proposes points where the ratio favours "good".
  • Multi-fidelity / early stopping of trials: evaluate many configurations cheaply (few epochs, a data subset, few boosting rounds) and only continue promising ones: successive halving, Hyperband, Optuna pruners. Within a single run, early stopping on a validation metric chooses the number of trees or epochs automatically.

Practical recipe for a GBDT: fix a moderately high learning rate (0.05–0.1) with early stopping; tune tree complexity (depth/num_leaves, min child weight), subsampling and regularisation with 50–200 Optuna trials; then lower the learning rate and retrain with early stopping for the final model.

3.13 Interpretability: global and local

Interpretability methods answer two kinds of question. Global: what does the model rely on overall, and how does the prediction change with a feature? Local: why did this customer get this score?

Built-in importances: linear coefficients (only comparable on standardised features, unstable under collinearity) and tree impurity importance (total impurity reduction from splits on a feature). Impurity importance is biased toward continuous and high-cardinality features, which offer more split points, and is computed on training data, so it can rank a random ID column highly.

Permutation importance: on validation data, shuffle one feature's column (breaking its relation to the target while keeping its distribution) and measure how much the score drops. Model-agnostic and measured where it matters (generalisation). Caveats: correlated features share credit (shuffle one, its twin still carries the signal, so both look unimportant), and shuffling creates unrealistic rows (a 5-year-old with a mortgage).

Partial dependence plots (PDP): set a feature to value v for every row, average the predictions, repeat across v, and plot. It shows the average marginal effect: "predicted price rises steeply up to 120 m², then flattens". ICE plots draw one line per row instead of the average, revealing heterogeneity (interactions) that the average hides. Both assume you can vary the feature independently of others, which is unrealistic for correlated features; accumulated local effects (ALE) plots address this.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) distributes a single prediction among features fairly, borrowing the Shapley value from cooperative game theory. Intuition: the features are players cooperating to move the prediction from the average (base value) to this example's output. A feature's Shapley value is its average marginal contribution when added to every possible coalition of the other features, averaged over all orderings. The values add up exactly: base value + Σ SHAP values = prediction. Example: a model's average prediction is 0.10 default probability (in log-odds space it is additive; we show probabilities for intuition); for one applicant, SHAP gives debt_ratio +0.15, late_payments +0.08, income −0.05, tenure −0.02, so the prediction is 0.26. For trees, TreeSHAP computes exact values in polynomial time; for other models KernelSHAP approximates by sampling. Aggregating |SHAP| over many rows gives a global importance; SHAP dependence plots show effect versus value.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations): perturb the example many times, get the black-box predictions, weight the perturbed samples by proximity to the original, and fit a simple interpretable model (sparse linear) to those local samples. Its coefficients explain the neighbourhood. Fast and flexible (works for text and images), but explanations depend on the perturbation scheme and kernel width and can be unstable between runs.

Explanations explain the model, not the world

A large SHAP value for postcode means the model uses postcode, not that postcode causes default. Interpretability tools are for debugging and accountability; causal claims need causal methods (experiments, causal inference).

3.14 Fairness basics

A model can be accurate overall and systematically worse, or harsher, for a group. Fairness evaluation slices metrics by a sensitive attribute (or a proxy where legally permitted) and compares:

  • Demographic parity: equal positive-prediction (selection) rates across groups. "Approve 30% in each group."
  • Equal opportunity: equal true positive rates (recall) across groups. "Qualified applicants are approved at the same rate."
  • Equalised odds: equal TPR and equal FPR across groups.
  • Predictive parity / calibration within groups: a score of 0.3 means a 30% outcome rate in every group.

A key result (Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan 2016; Chouldechova 2017): when base rates differ between groups, a non-trivial classifier cannot simultaneously satisfy calibration within groups and equal FPR and FNR. Fairness therefore requires choosing which criterion fits the decision's harms, which is a product, legal and ethical decision, not a purely technical one. Technical mitigations exist (reweighting, constraints during training, group-specific thresholds via Fairlearn's ThresholdOptimizer), and the first step is always measuring. Simply removing the sensitive attribute ("fairness through unawareness") rarely works, because proxies (postcode, name, purchase patterns) remain.

4. Internal Working

4.1 How ROC and PR curves are computed

  1. Sort examples by score descending.
  2. Walk down the list; at each distinct score, treat it as the threshold and update cumulative TP and FP counts (O(m log m) for the sort, O(m) for the sweep).
  3. ROC points: (FP/N, TP/P). PR points: (TP/P, TP/(TP + FP)).
  4. ROC-AUC by the trapezoidal rule, equivalently the Mann-Whitney U statistic: AUC = (sum of positive ranks − P(P + 1)/2)/(P · N). Average precision as Σ(Rn − Rn−1)Pn (scikit-learn's step-wise definition, which avoids the optimistic linear interpolation of PR curves).

4.2 What cross_validate does

For each split from the CV iterator: clone the estimator (unfitted copy with the same hyperparameters), fit on the training indices, score on the validation indices with each scorer, record fit and score times, optionally keep the fitted estimator. Jobs run in parallel via joblib. Scorers follow a "greater is better" convention, which is why scikit-learn exposes neg_mean_squared_error.

4.3 Inside an Optuna study

  1. The study asks its sampler for a trial; the objective function calls trial.suggest_*, which the sampler answers (TPE: split past trials into the top γ fraction "good" and the rest "bad", fit a Parzen density to each, sample candidates from the good density and pick the one maximising the good/bad density ratio).
  2. The objective trains and reports intermediate values (trial.report(score, step)); the pruner (median, successive halving, Hyperband) compares them with other trials at the same step and raises TrialPruned for unpromising ones.
  3. The completed value is stored (in memory, SQLite or an RDBMS, enabling distributed workers sharing one study).

4.4 How TreeSHAP gets exact Shapley values fast

Naively, Shapley values need the model's expected output for every subset of features: 2n subsets. For a tree, the expected output given a subset S can be computed by walking the tree: at a split on a feature in S, follow the example's branch; at a split on a feature not in S, follow both branches weighted by the fraction of training samples (cover) going each way. TreeSHAP tracks, in a single recursive pass, how many subsets of each size reach each leaf, giving exact values in O(T · L · D²) for T trees, L leaves and depth D, instead of exponential time. The "interventional" variant instead uses a background dataset to break feature dependence; the two can give different values for correlated features.

4.5 How permutation importance is computed

Score the fitted model on validation data (baseline). For each feature j and each of n_repeats: copy the data, permute column j, rescore, record the drop. Report mean and standard deviation of drops. Cost: n_features × n_repeats predictions over the validation set, no retraining.

5. Architecture: The Evaluation System

What to try: start at degree 1 and observe both errors high and close together (underfitting, high bias). Raise the degree step by step: training error falls monotonically, while test error falls, bottoms out, then climbs as the curve starts threading through individual noisy points (overfitting, high variance). Find the degree minimising test error. If the widget lets you resample the data or change the number of points, do so at a high degree: the fitted curve changes wildly between samples (that visible wobble is variance), and with more points the same degree overfits less, which is why more data reduces variance.

Reading the diagram: the test set is split off first and touched once. The inner tuning loop only ever sees cross-validation scores on the train+validation portion. Offline evaluation produces more than a single number (intervals, segment slices, calibration), and the gate compares against the current production model on the same test set. Passing offline is necessary but not sufficient: online testing is the final gate, mirroring a canary release.

Reading the diagram: stratified k-fold assumes production rows are exchangeable with training rows. Group k-fold assumes production brings new entities, so it keeps entities from leaking across the split. Time-series splitting assumes the future may differ from the past, so validation always comes after training, and expanding windows show how performance evolves as data accumulate. Pick the scheme whose assumption matches deployment.

Reading the diagram: global tools find what the model depends on (and expose leaks); effect plots check that dependence has a sensible shape; local tools justify individual decisions; fairness slicing checks that errors are distributed acceptably. Each feeds a different consumer, from the ML engineer to a compliance team.

6. The Math

6.1 Deriving the bias–variance decomposition

Setup: y = f(x) + ε with E[ε] = 0, Var(ε) = σ², ε independent of the training set D. The model f̂D depends on the random training set. Fix x and write f = f(x), f̂ = f̂D(x), f̄ = ED[f̂].

Step 1 (separate noise): E[(y − f̂)²] = E[(f + ε − f̂)²] = E[(f − f̂)²] + 2E[ε(f − f̂)] + E[ε²]. The cross term is zero because ε is independent of f̂ and has mean zero. So E[(y − f̂)²] = E[(f − f̂)²] + σ².

Step 2 (separate bias and variance): add and subtract f̄: E[(f − f̂)²] = E[(f − f̄ + f̄ − f̂)²] = (f − f̄)² + 2(f − f̄)E[f̄ − f̂] + E[(f̄ − f̂)²]. The middle term vanishes since E[f̂] = f̄. Hence

\[ \mathbb{E}_{D,\varepsilon}\big[(y-\hat f_D(\mathbf{x}))^2\big] = \underbrace{(f(\mathbf{x})-\bar f(\mathbf{x}))^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_D\big[(\hat f_D(\mathbf{x})-\bar f(\mathbf{x}))^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \sigma^2. \]

Numeric example: true f(x₀) = 10, noise σ² = 4. Across five training sets, a model predicts 12, 13, 11, 12, 12 at x₀: f̄ = 12, bias² = 4, variance = (0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0)/5 = 0.4, expected error 4 + 0.4 + 4 = 8.4. A more flexible model predicts 7, 14, 9, 13, 7: f̄ = 10, bias² = 0, variance = (9 + 16 + 1 + 9 + 9)/5 = 8.8, expected error 12.8. The unbiased model is worse.

6.2 Ridge regression: bias and variance in closed form

For a single standardised feature with Σx² = S, OLS ŵ = Σxy/S has variance σ²/S and no bias. Ridge ŵλ = Σxy/(S + λ) = ŵ · S/(S + λ): its mean is w · S/(S + λ), so bias = −wλ/(S + λ), and its variance is σ²S/(S + λ)². Example: S = 10, σ² = 4, w = 1, λ = 2: OLS MSE = 0.4; ridge bias² = (2/12)² = 0.028, variance = 40/144 = 0.278, total 0.306. A little bias bought a larger variance reduction.

6.3 ROC-AUC as a probability

\[ \text{AUC} = P(s^+ > s^-) + \tfrac12 P(s^+ = s^-) = \frac{1}{PN}\sum_{i\in +}\sum_{j\in -}\Big[\mathbb{1}(s_i > s_j) + \tfrac12\mathbb{1}(s_i = s_j)\Big]. \]

Any strictly increasing transformation of scores leaves every comparison unchanged, proving AUC ignores calibration.

6.4 Why precision collapses with rare positives

With prevalence π, recall R and false positive rate F, Bayes' rule gives

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{R\,\pi}{R\,\pi + F(1-\pi)}. \]

π = 0.001, R = 0.9, F = 0.01: precision = 0.0009/(0.0009 + 0.00999) ≈ 8.3%. Halving F to 0.005 gives 15.3%. Tiny FPR changes, invisible in ROC-AUC, nearly double precision.

6.5 Proper scoring rules

A scoring rule is proper if the expected score is optimised by reporting the true probability. For the Brier score with true probability q and report p: E = q(1 − p)² + (1 − q)p², derivative −2q(1 − p) + 2(1 − q)p = 2(p − q) = 0 ⇒ p = q. Log-loss is likewise proper. Accuracy at a threshold is not, so optimising it can reward miscalibrated probabilities.

6.6 Shapley values

\[ \phi_j = \sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!\,(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\Big[v(S\cup\{j\}) - v(S)\Big], \]

where v(S) is the expected model output when only the features in S are known. The weight is the fraction of feature orderings in which exactly the set S precedes j. Shapley values are the unique attribution satisfying efficiency (they sum to v(N) − v(∅)), symmetry, dummy (a feature that never changes the output gets 0), and additivity. Worked example: two features, v(∅) = 10, v({a}) = 30, v({b}) = 20, v({a, b}) = 50. Orderings a-then-b: a adds 20, b adds 20; b-then-a: b adds 10, a adds 30. φa = (20 + 30)/2 = 25, φb = (20 + 10)/2 = 15; sum 40 = 50 − 10. The interaction (50 − 30 − 20 + 10 = 10) is split equally.

6.7 Standard error of a cross-validation estimate

With fold scores s₁…sk, the naive standard error sd/√k underestimates uncertainty because folds share training data (scores are correlated). Treat it as a rough lower bound; for model comparisons, use the same folds for both models and look at the distribution of paired differences, or use repeated CV with a corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio, 2003).
